# Creative moves

Use a move when it opens a relevant possibility or helps with a particular block. Each is an optional exploration with an exit back to the task. You can switch midway, combine compatible moves, or stay with a productive fragment. The examples are invented illustrations, not study findings or evidence that a technique works.

## Begin with a particular moment

Use when the brief is so broad that every answer sounds like a category description.

Choose a concrete situation, action, material, or encounter. Describe what happens before naming a solution. Notice a mismatch, pause, unexpected detail, or competing intention. Follow the detail that changes what you want to make. If the situation is imagined, keep its status clear.

For an imagined invitation to a neighborhood dinner, begin with someone arriving late and recognizing a voice before seeing the table. That detail might suggest an invitation built around remembered voices, a sequence of introductions, or a shared place setting. Develop the direction that serves the desired welcome; the scene is a source of possibilities, not user research.

Return with the specific feature worth preserving. If the scene merely repeats the brief, change the situation or use actual material. Specificity is useful when it changes the idea.

## Follow an association without forcing a connection

Use when the available directions feel too close to the obvious answer, or when the user explicitly invites free exploration.

Start with a detail that catches attention. Follow an associated shape, action, contrast, memory from supplied material, or question. Permit a few changes of subject without requiring an application at each turn. Capture a promising fragment in its rough form. Then revisit the original question and see whether any relationship transfers.

For a fictional harbor exhibition, a frayed rope could lead to knots, interrupted paths, promises, or a record of departures. One of those associations might become a spatial arrangement or a narrative device. It may also lead nowhere. Keep an association only when its form, tension, or meaning earns a place in the work.

Use actual source details when available. Do not invent a personal memory or lived experience for the agent. End the detour when it repeats, loses interest without yielding material, or reaches the task's budget. No connection is an acceptable result.

## Change the question

Use when the stated problem already assumes a solution or when every answer optimizes the same thing.

Identify what the framing makes easy to see and what it leaves out. Rephrase the question through a cause, relationship, time scale, or desired experience. Keep the original beside the alternative. Generate a consequence of each framing before judging whether the change is useful.

An imagined museum brief asks how to make visitors spend longer in a room. An alternative asks what visitors should notice before leaving. The second might suggest a short encounter with a meaningful detail instead of more content. This is a proposed shift in purpose; it needs the owner's agreement if the original duration target is a fixed requirement.

Return with what the new question permits, what it sacrifices, and whether the actual goal still holds. Rewording without changing the possibilities is not a useful reframe. If missing facts determine the framing, investigate rather than invent an answer.

## Separate the job from the familiar form

Use when a genre convention, interface pattern, object, or first idea seems unavoidable.

Name what the familiar form accomplishes. Temporarily remove the form while keeping that job or expressive effect. Explore a different relationship, process, material behavior, or sequence that could accomplish it. Restore the real constraints and adapt what remains useful.

For a fictional archive installation, labels provide orientation and authority. Removing conventional labels might lead to visitors discovering provenance through drawers, traces, or successive reveals. Those alternatives change the encounter. If accessibility or attribution requires readable labels, preserve those requirements and relocate the discovery to another part of the design.

This move can expose a new form without treating all conventions as defects. Keep the original when it serves the work better. Do not ban first ideas or familiar patterns categorically.

## Transfer a relationship from another domain

Use when a different field may offer an operation or relation missing from the current approach.

Choose a source with something relevant to how the problem behaves: allocating scarce capacity, revealing information, creating anticipation, repairing damage, or coordinating parts. Describe that relation without relying on the source's appearance. Map the relevant parts to the target, adapt the relation, and check whether the conditions that make it work also hold in the target.

An imagined community tool library could borrow the idea of a musical rest: an intentional interval that makes a sequence possible. Applied structurally, it suggests planned gaps for tool maintenance between loans. Decorating the service with musical imagery is a different choice. The operational proposal still needs checks about demand, maintenance needs, and scheduling.

Distance alone is no reason to prefer an analogy. A near source can be useful; a distant one can be forced. If a source is factually unfamiliar, verify its mechanism before relying on it. Avoid treating an elegant analogy as proof that the target will work.

## Translate into another form

Use when language is hiding an arrangement, rhythm, dependency, movement, or material quality.

Choose what the new form should expose. Make a small translation and inspect it. Identify what appeared, what disappeared, and what may be an artifact of the translation. Bring the useful relation back into the requested medium.

| Question being explored | Possible translation | Return question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What feels crowded or disconnected? | Spatial diagram, collage, or arrangement of parts. | Which adjacencies or absences should change? |
| Where does attention rise, pause, or release? | Timeline, rhythm notation, or audio sketch if available. | What should repeat, interrupt, accelerate, or remain quiet? |
| Who acts, waits, exchanges, or changes? | Scene, storyboard, interaction sequence, or role arrangement. | Which relationship or decision was missing? |
| What moves, resists, folds, accumulates, or disappears? | Material study, described movement, or small physical/visual model. | Which behavior can shape the concept? |
| What causes the outcome? | Causal diagram, rules, or minimal code experiment. | Which dependency or feedback relation can change? |
| What varies and what must remain? | A small arrangement of parameters and alternatives. | Which combinations are coherent and worth developing? |

For an imagined sequence of poster panels, describe the timing as a short rhythmic pattern with a long pause. Translate that pause into an empty panel or delayed reveal. If you have only text, call it a rhythm notation; do not imply that you listened to a score. The direction succeeds only if the resulting visual sequence fits the experience being made.

Changing media can add confusion or cost. Stop if the translation supplies no new information. Avoid building a finished interactive artifact when a sketch answers the question.

## Use sensory qualities as design material

Use when the work is generic because it names themes without expressing a particular experience.

Start with a quality available in an inspected artifact or explicitly imagined study: roughness, translucency, echo, warmth, weight, interruption, density, or distance. Ask what happens when that quality changes. Explore its expressive or functional consequences, rather than compiling an inventory of senses.

For an imagined reading room, translucency could suggest partially visible material, gradual discovery, or layered views. Decide which of those belongs to the intended experience. A claim about actual visibility, comfort, acoustics, or accessibility requires the relevant evidence or a check; a verbal concept cannot establish those properties.

Use audio, image, video, or material evidence only through available access. Captions, transcripts, and descriptions supply different information from the underlying media. Where a sense cannot be inspected, work with a named abstraction and keep that limit visible when it matters.

## Play with a constraint, reversal, or chance cue

Use when removing every limitation has left the search vague, or when an assumption needs to become visible.

Choose a temporary rule that changes the search: reverse an exchange, remove a central element, change scale, limit a material, begin at the ending, or take an arbitrary cue. Keep fixed requirements intact. If you suspend a preference, mark it as suspended for exploration. If a rule only bans something, name a positive direction to explore. Try the consequence before deciding whether to retain the rule.

For a fictional welcome gift, a rule that nothing new may be manufactured might suggest a shared object, a repaired item, an invitation, or borrowed space. Those directions need not all be pursued. If manufacturing is a fixed part of the real brief, return any useful relation to that constraint before recommending it.

A chance cue can interrupt habit without carrying evidence or hidden authority. Let it suggest a possible move; discard it if the connection needs more explanation than the result warrants. Do not keep piling on constraints to demonstrate effort. A deliberately impossible sketch can reveal a desire, but its eventual feasibility remains a separate question.

## Recombine without averaging

Use when existing fragments contain complementary strengths.

Name the distinctive feature of each fragment. Combine through a specific relationship: one supplies the structure, another changes the point of view; one creates a rule, another creates an exception; one establishes a pattern, another interrupts it. Trace what the combination makes possible and where the parts conflict.

For an imagined local-history experience, a chronological map and an oral-history scene could combine by letting a place reveal different voices at different dates. The connection supplies a new encounter. Adding a map, a quiz, a timeline, and every collected interview without a governing relationship creates accumulation rather than a developed concept.

Keep alternatives apart when they depend on incompatible meanings or experiences. The goal is a coherent new direction, not inclusion of every fragment.

## Preserve the unusual part while developing it

Use when critique favors an ordinary alternative because the more distinctive one is still rough.

State what is worth keeping in the unusual direction. Identify its actual weakness: a hard conflict, unclear meaning, missing evidence, difficult production, or poor expression. Change the part responsible for the weakness while keeping the valuable quality. Make a small version when it will reveal whether that quality survives.

An imagined exhibition proposes that empty spaces mark objects that cannot be shown. If the absence is meaningful but visitors may miss it, explore how spacing, a trace, or a concise caption could make it perceptible. Filling every gap with a generic replacement removes the defining idea. Whether absence is appropriate depends on the actual subject and audience.

Compare the developed direction with the baseline at a similar level of detail. Keep it if its distinct contribution matters to the work. Release it when repair would erase its contribution or a fixed requirement makes it unsuitable; retain a useful fragment only if it still has a place.

## Bring a detour into different kinds of work

Choose the kind of return that the task needs. These invented examples show how the same freedom can lead to different next actions.

| Work | Exploratory change | Useful return |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A short story about estrangement. | Arrange its scenes as moments of approach and withdrawal before choosing a narrator. | Draft one scene whose viewpoint changes what the reader knows. Keep the emotional tension; judge the voice and scene, rather than whether the outline sounds novel. |
| A product concept for sharing equipment. | Look at exchanges as cycles of use, repair, and availability instead of a list of screens. | Describe a service arrangement that changes one of those relationships. Mark unknown demand or maintenance assumptions for research before claiming viability. |
| A technical workflow that keeps producing bottlenecks. | Draw where work accumulates and explore an alternative order or allocation, temporarily setting aside the existing component names. | State a candidate change and its affected invariants. Return it to the owning technical task to inspect against the real system; the diagram and analogy establish no performance gain. |
| A scientific explanation with competing causes. | Represent the accounts as different causal relationships and imagine what would distinguish them. | Return a hypothesis and a possible discriminating observation. Check existing literature and actual data; a coherent imagined explanation supplies no empirical finding. |
| An abstract visual piece. | Let a single material behavior or rhythm organize a rough study without requiring it to convey a literal message. | Compare the study's formal or expressive effect with the intended exploration. Keep ambiguity when it belongs to the work; a utilitarian account need not be added. |

## Resume after a genuine pause

Use when work is already being paused or handed back, or when a human collaborator chooses an incubation break.

Leave a concise note containing the live question, fixed requirements, current attempt, the repeating assumption, and any fragment worth recovering. On return, read that note before continuing. Try a different representation or starting condition when the old approach immediately repeats. Compare the resulting material with the previous attempt.

For an agent, the changed operation is the intervention. Elapsed time alone does not establish improvement, and unrelated busywork should not be added to mimic a human break. For a person, an uncomplicated activity after initial engagement is an option to try; no universal duration or guaranteed creative outcome is implied.

End with something the task can use: a candidate, a changed question, a developed fragment, or a clear account of what remains unresolved. Resume ordinary investigation or production when that is the useful next action.
