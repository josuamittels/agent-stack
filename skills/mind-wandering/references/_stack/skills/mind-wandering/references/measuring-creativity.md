# Measuring creativity across tasks

**Research brief · 27 October 2026**

## Short answer

There is no defensible, context-free creativity score. Assess creativity as a profile of qualities tied to a defined task, audience, comparison set, and time horizon. For most tasks, measure originality and task-relevant value separately, then report an overall judgment only if the decision requires one. Add meaning, aesthetic impact, surprise, or other dimensions when they are part of what success means for that task.

This follows the common view that creative work combines originality with effectiveness or value, while recognizing that these terms change meaning across contexts. A surprising use for a brick, a useful engineering prototype, a student’s new understanding, and a scientific finding cannot be judged against one identical rubric. Studies also find that the balance between novelty and usefulness shifts with task context. In one large study of verbal and figural divergent-thinking responses, novelty was a stronger predictor of overall creativity ratings than usefulness, but usefulness mattered more among ideas already judged highly novel. That is a reason to retain the dimensions in the data rather than assume a universal weighting. ([Diedrich et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0038688); [Plucker et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3902_2))

## A useful measurement frame

Before choosing a rubric, specify five things:

1. **Unit:** an idea, finished artifact, design proposal, moment in a process, learner’s insight, or a person’s accumulated achievement.
2. **Reference group:** what the output must be original relative to: the creator’s earlier work, the other responses in this session, current practice, or the domain’s historical record.
3. **Audience:** whose judgment matters: trained practitioners, intended users, peers, the creator, or a broader community.
4. **Purpose:** what the score will inform: learning, selection, design iteration, publication, adoption, or research.
5. **Time horizon:** whether value means immediate feasibility, later influence, personal learning, or durable contribution.

These choices define what the dimensions mean. “Original” can mean new to this learner, unusual in a small response pool, or unprecedented in a field. “Useful” can mean feasible under engineering constraints, relevant to a user’s need, or effective at conveying an artistic intention. The scoring guide should state the frame in plain language. The creativity literature has long warned that divergent-thinking tests measure selected parts of creative potential, not creativity in every sense or domain; the chosen task and the creator’s relevant expertise matter. ([Plucker et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3902_2); see also [Kaufman et al., 2008](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/humanities-social-sciences/psychology-12ps/clinical-psychology-12ps7/assessment-12ps77/essentials-of-creativity-assessment-978-0-470-13742-0))

| Dimension | What to judge | Practical operationalization | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Originality / novelty** | How far the output departs from familiar or existing alternatives in the stated reference group | Have raters judge unusualness and conceptual distance; where suitable, compare with a well-defined response pool or historical corpus | Treating rare or nonsensical responses as automatically original; scores change with sample size and fluency |
| **Relevance / appropriateness** | Whether the output addresses the prompt, audience, context, and constraints | Score task alignment, constraint satisfaction, and fit separately; specify hard requirements | Rewarding novelty that ignores the task, or letting polish mask irrelevance |
| **Usefulness / effectiveness / value** | Whether the output can achieve its intended function or create value for its intended audience | Use domain criteria such as feasibility, effectiveness, usability, or adoption; ask users or practitioners when their judgment is the target | Equating value with practical utility in art, learning, or expressive work |
| **Meaning / significance** | Whether the output communicates, interprets, or contributes something that matters within a defined context | Use audience interpretation, explanatory notes, and contextual expert judgment; preserve more than one defensible reading when relevant | Treating consensus as the only valid meaning or confusing personal liking with significance |
| **Surprise** | How unexpected the output is relative to a stated expectation | Ask a relevant audience what they expected before exposure, or compare with a predictive baseline; record whether surprise is valued | Treating surprise as novelty, or rewarding shock and randomness without fit or value |

Novelty and usefulness are not always equally weighted by raters. In a study of 1,500 participants’ verbal and figural responses, usefulness predicted creativity mainly among highly novel ideas. The result supports a two-stage diagnostic interpretation for some idea-generation tasks: first ask whether an idea is meaningfully unusual; then ask whether it works. It does not establish a universal formula, and it should not be carried unchanged into tasks where feasibility, safety, or user value is the main criterion. ([Diedrich et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0038688))

Rarity alone is a weak originality measure. A one-off answer may be a typo, a misunderstanding, or random nonsense. In divergent-thinking tasks, frequency scoring is also dependent on the size and composition of the response pool, while producing more answers raises the chance of producing an infrequent one. In their top-two method, Silvia and colleagues ask participants to select their two strongest responses and have independent raters score those responses; their study found dependable scores for unusual-uses and instances tasks with two or three raters. For broad verbal samples, computational semantic distance can help rank or screen responses, but it measures distance in a text model, not task-fit, usefulness, or creativity as a whole. It should be validated against human ratings in the target language and task. ([Silvia et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/1931-3896.2.2.68); [Beaty & Johnson, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-020-01453-w))

## Match the assessment to the task

| Task or decision | Recommended evidence | What the result can support |
|---|---|---|
| **Divergent thinking or brainstorming** | Use several prompts; retain fluency as a separate quantity measure; use blinded human ratings for the most promising responses or a validated originality rubric. Record task-specific appropriateness where the prompt has a purpose. | A bounded estimate of idea-generation performance under those prompts. Do not infer general creative ability from one alternate-uses prompt. |
| **Convergent creative problem solving** | Score constraint satisfaction and solution effectiveness alongside originality. Let domain experts judge technical plausibility; use users for usability or value when relevant. | Which solutions are both distinctive and viable for the stated problem. Hard safety or legal conditions may be thresholds, not qualities that can be averaged away. |
| **Art, writing, music, and other expressive products** | Use a consensual assessment technique: several independent judges familiar with the form rate anonymized work in random order. Ask for originality and form-specific qualities, such as craft, coherence, emotional or aesthetic effect, and intended communication. | Comparative judgments within the defined body of work and audience. Record disagreement and distinctive interpretations; consensus does not make one taste universal. |
| **Design concepts and prototypes** | Evaluate at the stage being tested. For concepts, assess novelty and value separately; for prototypes, include function, usability, and constraints. Pair expert assessment with representative user responses when adoption matters. | A stage-appropriate view of creative promise. A speculative idea and a tested product should not be scored as if they have equal evidence of effectiveness. |
| **Learning and classroom work** | Separate personal insight or “mini-c” progress from public originality. Compare work with the learner’s prior understanding and the assignment’s learning goals; use a content-specific rubric. | Evidence of creative learning without requiring a student to outperform a field or peer group. |
| **Creative achievement or selection** | Use evidence across multiple tasks or artifacts, relevant domain experience, and documented achievement; triangulate self-report with peer, expert, or outcome evidence. | A more defensible estimate of sustained, domain-specific performance than a brief laboratory task alone. |

The consensual assessment technique is particularly useful when the target is a real product rather than a presumed underlying trait. Its central test is whether appropriate judges independently converge in their ratings. Keep judges independent, remove identifying information when possible, rate work in randomized order, and use judges who understand the kind of product. Then report inter-rater agreement and the range of judgments, not just the mean. This method yields comparative judgments within the rated set; it does not by itself create a universal scale that can compare unrelated domains or cohorts. ([Amabile, 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.43.5.997))

For a process or learning study, observe actions, decisions, and revisions rather than relying only on the final artifact. Define the unit of analysis and coding rules for the activity: a brainstorm’s sequence of ideas and a learner’s developing explanation do not have the same meaningful “turn.” This kind of process score answers a different question from product quality, so report the two separately when both matter.

## Meaning and surprise need explicit reference points

Meaning is not interchangeable with usefulness. A poem, a lesson insight, or a scientific explanation may matter because it changes interpretation, gives coherent expression to an experience, or reframes a question, even when it has no direct practical utility. If meaning is an intended outcome, define whose meaning is at issue. Ask the audience what they understood, what felt salient, or how the work changed their view; pair those responses with close reading or domain expertise. Report the evidence and range of interpretations rather than turning “meaningful” into a vague global impression. The creator’s own experience can be a legitimate criterion when the task concerns learning or self-expression, while external audiences are more appropriate when the task concerns public communication or reception. These are different outcomes and should be labeled accordingly. (See also the distinction between personal learning and public achievement in [Plucker et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3902_2).)

Surprise is also relative. A response can be surprising because it violates an audience’s expectation, because it is statistically unlikely under a model, or because it joins concepts that are normally distant. Those are not equivalent. State whose expectation is being measured and when it is measured. A practical method is to ask the target audience to predict likely outputs before seeing the candidate, then rate the candidate’s unexpectedness and its value separately. A model-based surprisal score may be useful for a narrowly specified corpus or generation system, but should be treated as a property of that model and context, not as a direct human creativity score. Recent computational work formalizes newness, value, and surprise as distinct criteria and tests the combination in chess and painting; this is promising for domain-specific benchmarks, but it does not settle a general-purpose human measure. ([Yang & Tuzhilin, 2026](https://doi.org/10.24963/ijcai.2026/412))

## Build a score that can be trusted

- **Rate components separately before asking for a holistic judgment.** Collect originality, relevance, usefulness, meaning, and surprise only when they matter to the decision. A holistic “creativity” rating is useful as an outcome measure, but cannot show why an output succeeded or failed.
- **Use behaviorally anchored scales.** Describe what low, middle, and high scores look like for this prompt and audience. For originality, anchors might distinguish conventional, uncommon-but-familiar, and a genuinely new combination. For relevance, specify which requirements count. Pilot the anchors on real examples.
- **Use multiple tasks and raters.** A task score is partly a property of the prompt; a rating is partly a property of the judge. Estimate reliability across tasks and raters where the decision warrants it. A 2026 systematic quantitative review identified more than 110 divergent-thinking administration and scoring variations across 451 studies, and found substantial heterogeneity and underreporting. This makes transparent instructions, scoring rules, and task sampling essential for reproducibility. ([Saretzki et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/aca0000691))
- **Separate reliability from validity.** High agreement does not prove that judges assessed the intended construct. Check whether scores relate to external criteria that fit the purpose, such as task success, learning gains, user adoption, or later domain recognition. Use the appropriate standard of evidence for any high-stakes interpretation. ([Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing](https://www.testingstandards.net/open-access-files.html))
- **Keep the components visible.** Report score distributions, uncertainty, disagreement, and how scoring changed with task or audience. Avoid a single aggregate unless the user of the result needs a ranking or decision.
- **If an aggregate is necessary, choose the rule from the decision.** A weighted sum can represent trade-offs; a threshold or minimum rule can ensure a solution is both novel enough and relevant enough; an interaction can reflect that usefulness matters most once an idea passes an originality bar. Pre-register or document weights and thresholds, and show how rankings change under reasonable alternatives. Do not present an arbitrary multiplication of dimensions as a natural law.

## Conclusion

The most useful general approach is a **task-specific scorecard**, not a universal creativity test: define the unit, audience, comparison set, and purpose; score originality separately from relevance and task-specific value; add meaning or surprise only when the task requires them; and use multiple representative tasks and independent raters. That approach preserves what a creative result does well and reveals the tradeoffs a single score would conceal.

## Methods and limitations

This is a default-depth, narrative research brief answering how to assess originality, relevance, usefulness, meaning, and surprise across task types. The search was conducted on 27 October 2026. I attempted OpenAlex API discovery (`search.title_and_abstract=creativity assessment originality usefulness`) and its website search; the API hostname could not be resolved from this environment and the website was not accessible through the available search tool. I therefore used web searches for focused queries on creativity assessment, consensual assessment, divergent-thinking scoring, task specificity, product evaluation, and surprise, followed by inspection of accessible article pages, abstracts, and full-text versions where offered. Results are a bounded fallback search, not a comprehensive OpenAlex search or systematic review.

The default journal-selection rule was retained: include journal articles when the repository’s supplied CSV gives Article Influence ≥ 2.5 **or** influence percentile ≥ 80, with any qualifying category row sufficient. The CSV does not state the metric year or percentile derivation, and venue scores were used only as a selection rule, not as study-quality evidence. Journal articles cited here were checked against that local rule; books, standards, and conference proceedings were considered separately. Access was limited for some publisher full texts, so those sources were assessed from accessible records or author versions. The strongest challenge to a universal score is the observed context sensitivity of novelty and usefulness ratings and the wide methodological variation in divergent-thinking tasks. This supports the report’s task-relative recommendation, while leaving the exact best weighting for any specific application unresolved.
