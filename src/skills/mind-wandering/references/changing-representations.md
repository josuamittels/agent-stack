# Changing representations: Can changing the medium reveal ideas?

**Evidence search:** 26 October 2026\
**Research depth:** Default, interdisciplinary narrative synthesis\
**Journal selection:** Default preset for journal articles (article influence ≥ 2.5 **or** influence percentile ≥ 80, inclusive). Books are retained as theory sources, not screened by journal metrics.

## Short answer

Yes, sometimes. Changing a representation can make a different feature of a problem easier to notice, manipulate, compare, or remember. A diagram can make spatial relations visible at once; a formula can expose operations that prose hides; a gesture can externalize a movement or relation while someone explains it. The strongest causal evidence in the sources reviewed here concerns gesture during learning and coordinated representations in STEM learning. These studies support the idea that representation changes can reveal or support thought, but they do not establish that switching media reliably produces more original ideas in every creative task.

The useful question is therefore not “Which medium is best?” but “Which feature does this representation make available, and what does the person have to do to connect it to the others?” Representation changes help when they supply a complementary view, make a relevant relation easier to inspect, or prompt a productive re-encoding. They can hinder when they add irrelevant detail, require unfamiliar translation, overload attention, or impose a form that does not fit the task. Evidence is much stronger for words, diagrams, symbols, and gesture than for sound or material manipulation as general-purpose ideation techniques.

## Question and scope

This report asks whether people can discover ideas or relations they miss by shifting between words, images, diagrams, sounds, movement, and physical materials. It treats “find ideas” broadly: solving a problem, learning or transferring a concept, and generating or extending candidate ideas. It does not treat these outcomes as interchangeable. A teaching experiment can show that learners acquire a concept more successfully; it does not by itself show that they become more creative in open-ended work.

“Changing representations” can mean at least three things: translating the same information between forms (for example, a sentence into a diagram), combining forms that contribute different information, or using the body or an object to enact a thought. The research does not always test an explicit switch from one medium to another. Many studies instead compare presentation conditions, or examine whether gestures accompany speech. That distinction matters: evidence that two linked formats help is not direct proof that a deliberate switching exercise will help every user.

## Findings

### A representation can make a different structure available

Representations are not neutral containers. Their form can make some operations or relationships perceptually easy and others hard. In Ainsworth’s DeFT framework, multiple representations may complement one another, constrain a misleading interpretation, or prompt learners to build a deeper understanding. Their effects depend on design choices and on the cognitive work required to coordinate them. Ainsworth’s account helps explain why adding a second or third form can help in one task and do little in another.

Experiments support this task-fit account. In a study of propositional-logic problems, students using formula and text representations together solved more problems than students relying on only one representation. The forms offered different affordances: prose supported meaningful interpretation, while notation compactly exposed logical operations. This is evidence for a complementary pair in a specific domain, not a general advantage of switching for every learner or task (Ott et al., 2018).

The same principle appears in spatial learning. Schüler, Pazzaglia, and Scheiter found that maps helped when learners needed spatial knowledge, but did not improve learning of non-spatial visual details. Overlap between text and maps was useful for spatial content, where the representations could guide each other. The medium helped when it expressed the target information in a form that made it easier to reason about; its mere presence was insufficient (Schüler et al., 2019).

This is consistent with distributed and extended accounts of cognition: people can use marks, objects, and environmental structure as part of a thinking process rather than treating them only as outputs of prior thought. Hutchins’ ethnographic account of navigation, and Norman’s analysis of everyday cognitive artifacts, offer useful explanations for why external structure can reduce the amount that must be kept mentally active. These are theoretical and observational accounts, not randomized tests of a creativity method.

### Gestures can externalize thought and sometimes help it develop

Gesture provides a particularly clear example of a representation that bridges bodily movement and language. In an experiment where adults and children explained math solutions while remembering words or letters, participants remembered more when they gestured than when they were asked to keep still. The authors interpreted this as gesture reducing the resources needed for explanation, leaving more capacity for the concurrent memory task (Goldin-Meadow et al., 2001). This supports a cognitive-offloading mechanism; it does not establish an increase in idea originality.

In a math-instruction experiment, children instructed to produce gestures expressing correct information learned more than children instructed to produce partially correct gestures, who learned more than children who made no gestures. The benefit was associated with children incorporating information conveyed in gesture into speech. Here, movement was not simply an outlet for a fully formed thought: it could contribute information that became part of what children could say and use (Goldin-Meadow et al., 2009). The result concerns learning a specific mathematical concept among children, so its reach to adult creative work remains uncertain.

Kirk and Lewis tested children’s generation of novel uses for everyday objects. Spontaneous gesture frequency correlated with creative fluency, while restricting gesture did not reduce fluency in the first experiment. In a second experiment, encouraging gesture increased the number of creative ideas. The randomized encouragement provides more direct evidence than the correlation, though the outcome was divergent-thinking performance on a constrained task rather than real-world creative output (Kirk & Lewis, 2017).

Together, the gesture studies suggest two plausible routes: movement can ease the working-memory demands of expressing a thought, and it can add or stabilize information that speech alone has not yet captured. But movement effects are not uniform. Whether a gesture is meaningful, whether it fits the task, and whether a person can use or interpret it are likely to matter. The studies do not support the simpler claim that moving more always produces better ideas.

### Multiple representations can help, but coordination is the active ingredient

The recent systematic review and meta-analysis of more than two external representations in STEM education synthesized 46 studies; 23 contributed to its meta-analysis. When learning conditions with more than two forms were compared with two forms, the pooled performance advantage was small (Hedges’ *g* = 0.117). Comparisons with a single representation were less conclusive in the more conservative multilevel analysis. Effects varied, and appropriate instructional support mattered. Only three included studies directly examined problem-solving rather than learning, making the review much stronger evidence about STEM instruction than about general ideation (Rexigel et al., 2024).

The review also found that extra representations could hurt performance in some settings, including for some secondary-school learners compared with a single representation. The authors discuss prior knowledge, support, informational overlap, and whether representations are presented together or sequentially as potential moderators. Since the underlying studies differ substantially and many report learning outcomes rather than open-ended problem solving, the pooled estimate should not be read as a universal “more is better” effect.

Rau, Aleven, and Rummel’s work on multiple graphical representations similarly emphasizes the need to connect representations rather than merely encounter them. Their intervention used self-explanation prompts to support learning across graphical depictions. In practice, translating between forms makes assumptions explicit: What in the drawing corresponds to this symbol? Which part of the explanation is not shown? What changes if the relation is reorganized? A representation switch is most useful when it prompts those comparisons, not when it simply adds another display.

### Changing form can loosen a fixed interpretation, but evidence is narrower

Insight research studies a related process called representational change: solvers revise the way they have framed a problem, often by relaxing an assumed constraint or decomposing a familiar grouping. Training experiments show that people can improve at certain verbal-insight problem categories when taught to identify inconsistencies between their interpretation and the problem statement. The gains were specific to the trained categories, which is a useful warning against assuming that a general “switch medium” instruction transfers broadly.

That literature is adjacent to, but not the same as, cross-modal switching. The insight studies establish that changing a mental representation can help solve particular problems; they do not show that drawing, movement, or sound is the cause of that change. Conversely, the multiple-representation learning studies show that forms can complement each other, but often do not measure sudden insight or novel idea generation. The two bodies of work make a coherent case for representation as an active part of thinking, while leaving the causal value of a deliberate multimodal ideation routine unsettled.

### The evidence for sound and physical materials is less direct

The research located for this brief does not support a broad claim that adding sound, music, or physical materials reliably uncovers ideas missed in words or diagrams. Material objects can make spatial or causal relationships available for inspection and manipulation, and sound can make timing or rhythm perceptible. Those are plausible task-specific affordances, but evidence from gesture or educational diagrams cannot be transferred to sound and tangible materials without testing. In this search, I found substantially more direct experimental evidence for gesture and text/diagram/symbol combinations than for sound-based or object-based methods as tools for idea generation.

## What helps, and what can get in the way

The evidence suggests a practical sequence for using representational shifts:

1. **Name what is stuck.** Is the obstacle a hidden spatial relation, a sequence, a constraint, a missing comparison, or a working-memory burden?
2. **Choose a form that exposes that feature.** Draw a layout for spatial relations, enact a movement for a process, or write a compact symbolic form for operations. A change of medium is useful only if it changes what can be noticed or done.
3. **Translate back and compare.** Ask what the new form makes visible, what it leaves out, and whether the original framing made an assumption seem necessary. The act of mapping between forms may be as important as either form by itself.
4. **Stop when the new form adds load without new information.** Multiple representations can compete for attention. Unfamiliar conventions, redundant content, poor alignment, or extra detail can make interpretation harder. Learner knowledge and task demands matter.

For open-ended creative work, treat these as low-cost prompts to test rather than established interventions: explain an idea in words, sketch its structure, enact the relation, or give it a temporal/sound pattern, then note what changed. Keep the prompt tied to the stuck feature and evaluate whether the new form yields a useful relation or candidate, not just a more vivid artifact. Research currently provides better support for this disciplined variation than for any specific universal sequence of media.

## Limits and conclusion

The answer is yes, with boundaries. Shifting forms can help people access relations that are awkward to represent in the starting medium, lower the burden of holding information in mind, or prompt a re-encoding of a fixed interpretation. The evidence is strongest for complementary text, diagram, and symbolic forms in learning, and for gesture in explanation, math learning, and constrained divergent-thinking tasks. The conditions matter: the new form should fit the information, be understandable to the user, and be connected back to the original problem.

The evidence does not establish that cycling through words, pictures, diagrams, sounds, movement, and physical objects is a general creativity booster. Studies often examine one medium at a time, focus on student learning, and use short laboratory tasks. Evidence on sound and object manipulation was sparse in the accessible search. More direct studies would compare a planned representational switch with staying in one form, measure the quality and novelty of resulting ideas, and test transfer beyond the specific task. For now, changing representation is best treated as a purposeful way to inspect a problem from a different angle, with benefits to be judged by what becomes newly visible.
