# English Prose Writing – Best Practices

Natural English makes the intended meaning available without unnecessary effort. Its words, sentences, and structure fit the subject, reader, and form. It can be formal, conversational, terse, analytical, or literary.

Preserve meaning, facts, necessary terminology, and the integrity of quotations. Apply the general conventions across forms. The style sections add requirements and narrowly necessary exceptions for academic, technical, absolute, article, and storytelling prose.

The punctuation and presentation conventions establish a consistent house style. They are editorial preferences rather than universal laws of English. A single word, sentence pattern, or punctuation mark does not establish authorship.

Examples illustrate writing choices. They do not report actual research findings, events, product behavior, or measurements.

## Meaning, reader, and register

### Preserve the proposition

Keep what a sentence says, who or what it concerns, and how strongly it says it. A smoother revision must preserve conditions, exclusions, quantities, causality, chronology, attribution, and uncertainty. Shortening a sentence is useful only if the reader receives the same relevant information.

| Original | Meaning-changing revision | Faithful revision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "The treatment may reduce symptoms in some patients." | "The treatment reduces symptoms." | "Some patients may experience fewer symptoms with the treatment." |
| "Only administrators can delete archived records." | "Administrators can delete records." | "Deleting an archived record requires administrator access." |
| "The team reported fewer errors after the change." | "The change prevented errors." | "After the change, the team reported fewer errors." |
| "He was tired and did not want to talk." | "Exhausted, he preferred silence." | "He was tired. He did not want to talk." |
| "This explanation may fit dogs that live with other dogs." | "The behavior is common in animals that live with other pets." | "The explanation may apply to dogs living with other dogs." |

Keep deliberate ambiguity where it belongs to a literary work. Remove accidental ambiguity in an explanation, instruction, or factual claim. A sentence that is intentionally open to interpretation differs from one whose pronoun has two possible referents.

### Write for the reader's actual knowledge

Give readers the concepts they need before relying on them. Explain an unfamiliar term near its first relevant use. Skip explanations of knowledge the intended audience can reasonably be expected to have.

Use concrete examples to introduce difficult ideas, then connect the example to the general point. An explanation should neither require unexplained specialist knowledge nor bury a knowledgeable reader in elementary definitions.

> Unhelpful: "Its idempotency ensures robust retries."
>
> Clearer for a general reader: "Repeating the request does not create another order, so the client can retry it."

The technical term may remain when the audience needs it. "The request is idempotent: repeating it does not create another order" teaches the term without making it carry the explanation alone.

Give the full form of an unfamiliar abbreviation at first use, followed by the abbreviation in the notation the form requires. Use the abbreviation consistently afterward. A short passage may be clearer with the full term alone. Familiarity depends on the intended readers, so a standard abbreviation in one field may need explanation for another audience.

An abstract, caption, table note, or other independently readable element needs enough definitions to make sense on its own. Define an unfamiliar abbreviation separately in the abstract and main text when readers may encounter either without the other.

### Match register to the form

Use formal language for scholarly claims, literal language for procedures, and character-appropriate language for fiction. Natural prose does not require slang, contractions, first-person anecdotes, rhetorical questions, or a friendly opening.

Contractions are ordinary in conversational prose. Formal academic prose generally uses their expanded forms. Dialogue may use contractions, dialect, and incomplete utterances because those features belong to speech. Keep spelling and usage consistent within the chosen variety of English, while preserving quotations, proper names, and established terms.

### Describe people accurately and respectfully

Use specific descriptions relevant to the subject. Respect a person's name, pronouns, and relevant identity preferences. Singular "they" is appropriate when gender is unknown, unspecified, or the person's stated usage. Do not infer identity from appearance, a name, or group membership.

Mention age, disability, ethnicity, nationality, sex, gender, or another personal characteristic when it contributes necessary information. Avoid stereotypes, demeaning labels, and wording that reduces a person to a diagnosis. Person-first and identity-first language both have legitimate uses. Follow the relevant person's or community's preference where known. A community is not necessarily unanimous.

Distinguish sex from gender where the distinction affects meaning. In research reporting, name what was actually recorded or measured rather than replacing one category with another. Preserve participants' self-descriptions and the limits of the recorded data.

> Vague: "The elderly struggled with the service."
>
> More specific, if supported: "Participants aged 70 to 84 reported difficulty reading the service's small text."

The revision requires those ages and reports to be established. Respectful language does not justify invented precision or euphemisms that obscure the finding.

### Preserve voice without preserving avoidable confusion

A recognizable voice can depend on restraint, expansiveness, dry humor, directness, or characteristic sentence movement. Preserve those meaningful features. Remove accidental repetition, faulty grammar, unsupported claims, and obscure phrasing that obstruct the intended effect.

When a mechanical edit makes a sentence less clear or changes its meaning, use another construction. Preserve an established, meaningful usage rather than replacing it solely to satisfy a stylistic preference.

Writing by multilingual authors can retain a distinctive order of emphasis, directness, or cadence while remaining clear English. Preserve those features when they communicate effectively. Correct an unclear construction without making the author sound colloquial, adding cultural references they did not choose, or replacing their perspective with a generic voice.

Do not introduce typos, random digressions, unnecessary rare words, arbitrary sentence-length changes, or weaker coherence to imitate a person. A polished sentence can be natural. An unpolished sentence can be formulaic.

## Evidence, specificity, and uncertainty

### State the concrete claim

Say what happened, what something does, what changes, or what the reader needs to know. Replace atmospheric reassurance with a mechanism, consequence, instruction, or supported number.

> Vague: "The new workflow delivers a seamless experience."
>
> Concrete: "The form keeps the entered values when submission fails."

The second sentence is appropriate only if that behavior is established. Specificity must come from the material, not from invented details.

### Separate observation, attribution, and interpretation

Distinguish an observed event from a reported event and from a conclusion about either. Identify whose judgment a statement expresses.

> "Three participants stopped before the final question" describes an observation.
>
> "The participants said that the final question was unclear" attributes an explanation.
>
> "The question may have contributed to the dropouts" offers an interpretation.

Do not silently turn sequence into causation, a person's impression into consensus, or a plausible account into an established fact.

An explanation that could fit a case is not evidence that the behavior is common. Preserve the population and the kind of claim. A narrower category such as "dogs" cannot become "animals," and a possibility cannot become an observed frequency, solely to smooth the prose.

### Name the source of a claim

Replace "experts believe," "research suggests," and "some critics argue" with an identified source and the relevant claim. If the source is unavailable, remove the attribution or state the uncertainty without pretending that evidence exists.

Named references are useful when the identity matters. They should clarify an argument, locate an influence, or provide a source. Dropping a name adds little if the reference is unexplained or irrelevant.

Attach a source to the claim it actually supports. A real reference can still be misused through an inaccurate paraphrase, a broader population, a missing condition, or a conclusion the source never reaches. Make the boundary between the source's findings and the writer's inference clear.

Distinguish a preprint, a published study, a dataset, and a secondary account of research. Publication status describes the source. It does not by itself establish the truth of a claim. Neither an author's reputation nor a citation count replaces relevant evidence.

Keep the title, authors, date, identifier, quoted wording, findings, and page or section locator consistent with the version actually cited. Do not combine a preprint's finding with a later article's bibliographic details when the versions differ. When comparing versions, attribute each claim to its own version.

### Make numerical comparisons interpretable

Identify the population, timeframe, units, denominator, and comparison baseline needed to understand a number. Name the quantity being counted or measured. A percentage of respondents differs from a percentage of all eligible people, and a total number of incidents differs from an incident rate.

| Distinction | Writing requirement | Illustrative wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Count and rate | Give counts with relevant population sizes or rates when comparing groups of different sizes. | "District A recorded 40 incidents among 10,000 residents. District B recorded 60 among 30,000." The rates are 4 and 2 incidents per 1,000 residents. |
| Percentage and percentage points | Distinguish relative change from the difference between two percentages. | A rise from 20% to 30% is 10 percentage points, or a 50% relative increase. |
| Mean and median | Name the summary used. Do not describe either as what everyone typically experiences without considering the distribution. | "Median waiting time was 12 minutes" identifies a different statistic from "Mean waiting time was 12 minutes." |
| Monetary value across time | State whether amounts are nominal or adjusted for inflation, and identify the reference year when relevant. | "Spending rose in nominal terms but fell when expressed in 2024 prices" requires evidence for both comparisons. |

Use comparable periods, geographic boundaries, definitions, and populations. Explain a material change in coverage or classification rather than presenting it as a change in the underlying phenomenon. Pair a relative change with its starting value or absolute change where scale affects interpretation. Avoid decimal precision that the measurement cannot support.

The arithmetic mean is the total divided by the number of observations. The median is the middle value in the ordered observations, or the midpoint of the two middle values when their number is even. Extreme values can move the mean substantially without a comparable change in the median. Specify the summary and describe relevant variation rather than using "average" ambiguously.

### Distinguish magnitude, precision, and importance

An effect's size, the precision of its estimate, its statistical significance, and its practical importance answer different questions. Describe the consequence in relevant units where possible. A small effect can be estimated precisely, and an important possible effect can remain uncertain.

Report an uncertainty interval with its level and type where relevant. Explain what the range means for the substantive claim, using an interpretation appropriate to the method. Do not treat a confidence interval as a range containing a stated proportion of individual observations.

A nonsignificant result does not establish that an effect is absent or that two treatments are equivalent. An uncertain estimate may remain compatible with materially different outcomes. Describe what the evidence can distinguish. An equivalence claim needs evidence suited to that claim.

> Overstated: "The groups did not differ."
>
> More bounded, for an inconclusive comparison: "The estimate was too imprecise to establish whether the groups differed meaningfully."

The bounded wording is appropriate only when imprecision explains the result. Do not substitute it automatically for every nonsignificant finding.

### Keep the level of inference clear

A relationship among regions, schools, or other groups does not establish the same relationship among their individual members. State the level at which the evidence was collected and the conclusion applies.

Aggregate results can conceal differences among subgroups or change when the proportions of those groups change. Explain a material discrepancy rather than switching between overall and subgroup findings to favor a conclusion. Do not invent subgroup patterns that the evidence does not establish.

Statistical adjustment alone does not establish causality. A plausible mechanism, a temporal sequence, or a predictive relationship also needs the appropriate evidence before it becomes a causal explanation. Consider relevant selection effects, confounding, reverse causation, and regression toward the mean when those alternatives bear on the interpretation. Report the design's actual inferential limits.

### Explain the step from evidence to conclusion

Make the reason a piece of evidence supports a claim intelligible. A quotation, statistic, or citation supplies material for an argument. The relationship to the conclusion may still need explanation. Identify a consequential assumption rather than leaving it hidden in a confident transition such as "therefore."

Represent competing arguments accurately. Include material counterevidence and explain whether differences in population, method, measurement, or setting account for disagreement. Do not select only favorable findings, dismiss criticism through personal attacks, or give unsupported positions equal evidential standing for the appearance of balance.

Distinguish a finding, a supported explanation, and a speculation. An observation compatible with an explanation does not uniquely establish it. A persuasive account should acknowledge a plausible alternative when it materially changes what the evidence warrants.

### Anchor time-sensitive claims

Use a date, period, or version where words such as "currently," "recently," and "latest" would become ambiguous or misleading. A statement about an earlier institutional role should retain "former" or its relevant dates when those determine the person's relationship to the subject.

Distinguish the date an event occurred from the date of a source reporting it. A later historical account can describe an earlier event, but the event cannot have been influenced by a document that did not yet exist. Do not infer an order of events from the order in which sources discuss them.

> Time-dependent: "The latest edition adds an exception."
>
> Anchored form: "The 2024 edition adds an exception to the 2020 rule."

The anchored wording requires those editions and the change between them to be established. A date is useful context only when it is accurate.

### Prefer evidence to evaluative adjectives

"Significant," "impressive," "powerful," and "effective" need a clear basis. Use the result or consequence when available. In statistical writing, reserve "statistically significant" for a defined statistical result.

> Unsupported: "The change significantly improves performance."
>
> Illustrative measurement: "In the benchmark, median processing time fell from 120 ms to 90 ms."

Identify the conditions relevant to the comparison. Where no measurement exists, describe the known behavior without implying a measured improvement.

### Preserve useful qualifications

Remove stacked uncertainty, not uncertainty itself. "May" often replaces "could potentially possibly." Keep the population, condition, timeframe, and limitation that determine whether the claim is true.

> Bloated: "It could potentially be argued that the effect might be temporary."
>
> Direct: "The effect may be temporary."

Do not replace "may" with "will" for confidence, or "in this sample" with a universal claim for brevity. Preserve relevant exceptions and competing explanations.

### Make examples carry information

An example should demonstrate the point, expose a limit, or distinguish similar cases. Follow it with explanation only where the connection would otherwise be unclear. Do not explain the same implication twice.

A single incident can illustrate a claim without establishing how widely it holds. Keep the distinction between an illustrative anecdote, a counterexample, and evidence for a general conclusion.

In factual writing, distinguish an illustrative scenario from an actual event. In fiction, invented detail belongs to the established story world and must remain consistent with it.

### Give quotations a purpose

Use a quotation when its particular wording supplies evidence, reveals a perspective, or matters to the argument. Introduce enough context to identify the speaker and the claim. Explain the connection where needed, rather than leaving the quotation to stand in for reasoning.

Paraphrase where the wording itself adds little. Preserve a quoted speaker's actual words, uncertainty, and scope. Do not invent speech or present an illustrative exchange as a reported conversation.

Attribute borrowed ideas as well as borrowed wording. A paraphrase should express the understood idea in a construction suited to the present argument. Replacing words with synonyms while retaining a source's distinctive sentence structure can remain an overly close paraphrase. Use a quotation when the original expression itself must remain.

Identify an indirect citation when the original work is known only through another source. Do not imply firsthand reading or attach a locator to a text that was not consulted. Supply a page, section, paragraph, timestamp, or other suitable locator for quotations where the form requires one. Clearly distinguish a translation from the source's original wording.

## Organization and paragraph development

### Begin with something useful

Open with the claim, situation, observation, event, or question that gives the piece a reason to exist. Remove greetings, broad declarations about the modern world, and announcements that the text will explore its subject.

> Padded: "In today's rapidly evolving landscape, understanding backups is more important than ever."
>
> Direct: "A backup is useful only if you can restore it."

An opening can be quiet. It needs relevance, not manufactured drama.

### Let the subject determine the structure

Arrange material around its purpose and dependencies. A procedure follows action order. An argument develops reasons. A reference supports lookup. A narrative controls experience and disclosure.

Avoid filling a prefabricated introduction, three equal sections, and a recap when the material needs a different shape. Use the number and length of sections that the subject requires.

### Give each paragraph a distinct contribution

A paragraph should establish a point, develop it, supply evidence, qualify it, connect it to another idea, or create a necessary narrative effect. Several sentences may belong to one paragraph when they develop the same movement.

Do not create a new paragraph merely because another sentence has been written. Do not keep a block intact when its subject, time, speaker, or argumentative function has changed.

Details can serve different purposes. One may establish context, another reveal a limitation, and another complicate the argument. They need not all repeat the central message.

Repeated explanation can appear to add depth while contributing no information. Replace it with the missing reason, example, condition, consequence, or alternative interpretation. If none is needed, end the paragraph.

### Connect ideas through their relationship

Show whether the next sentence follows, contrasts, qualifies, explains, or changes the subject. Use "because," "but," "after," and similar connectors when they express that relationship. Remove transition words that merely announce another sentence.

> Mechanical: "Additionally, the service stores logs. Furthermore, it records failures."
>
> Clearer: "The service records failures in its logs."

The revision is valid only if the two statements refer to the same behavior. Combining unrelated claims would change the meaning.

Simple transitions such as "then" and "next" are useful for actual sequences. Repeated "and" can blur independent claims, while repeated "then" can give every event the same weight. Combine, subordinate, or separate clauses according to their relationship. Variety alone is not a reason to choose a more elaborate transition.

### Develop enough, then stop

Concision means retaining what the reader needs in less unnecessary language. It does not mean reducing a substantial thought to a generic label.

"The book was interesting" may need an explanation of what interested the writer. A paragraph that already demonstrates the point may need no additional summary. Match the amount of development to the reader's task.

### Preserve complexity that belongs to the subject

Represent real exceptions, conflicting motives, uncertainty, and costs. Do not force a tidy binary distinction or universal lesson onto material that does not support one.

Keep conceptual complexity while reducing linguistic difficulty. A clear explanation may describe an unresolved problem. Clarity does not require resolving it.

### Avoid manufactured contrasts and false ranges

State the point without staging a rejected alternative that the reader has no reason to consider. "Not just X, but Y" often adds emphasis without adding a distinction. Keep a contrast when both sides matter, and explain the actual difference.

Use "from X to Y" for a meaningful span, scale, or progression. If the items are simply topics, name them directly. "From teamwork to innovation" does not establish a range.

### Let substance determine the wording

The strength of a piece depends on what it includes, omits, connects, and concludes. Replacing suspect words cannot repair an empty argument, a missing explanation, or an unearned narrative turn.

### End where the material has earned an ending

An ending may establish a consequence, answer the opening question, state the remaining uncertainty, or identify a justified next action. Omit a closing sentence that merely praises the future or repeats the introduction.

Remove generic closing claims such as "The future looks bright." Avoid uplifting resolutions, engagement questions, universal lessons, and confident recommendations added because the piece appears to need a conclusion. In a story, an image or action may finish the work without an explanatory moral.

## Sentence structure and syntax

### Give the sentence an identifiable subject and action

Keep the actor, action, and object easy to locate. Avoid long openings that postpone the main clause or separate a subject from its verb with several qualifications.

> Dense: "The configuration of the import budget, after consideration of the files currently included in the project, is performed through the configuration file."
>
> Clear: "The configuration file sets the import budget."

Retain the qualification separately if it contains a necessary fact. Remove it only when it repeats what the surrounding text already establishes.

### Express one main thought without imposing one sentence length

A sentence can carry a claim with its condition, reason, or consequence. Split it when it carries several competing claims or forces the reader to backtrack.

> Overloaded: "The server rejected the request, which was missing a token, and the client retried, although the account was inactive, which caused another failure."
>
> Clearer: "The server rejected the request because it lacked a token. The client retried, but the inactive account caused another failure."

Longer sentences are useful when their clauses belong together. A succession of clipped sentences can make a connected explanation harder to follow.

### Use active voice when the actor matters

Prefer "The loader parses the file" to "The file is parsed by the loader." Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or less important than the process or affected object.

Forms of "be" followed by a past participle can indicate passive voice, but they are not a reliable grammatical test on their own. "The door is closed" may describe a state. Do not rewrite every such construction mechanically.

Do not invent an actor to avoid the passive. In academic writing, a passive construction can appropriately keep attention on the method or specimen.

### Make clause relationships explicit

Use coordination for ideas of comparable weight and subordination for a condition, reason, concession, or dependency. Avoid chaining every event with "and" when the relationship is causal or temporal.

> Loose chain: "The battery was empty and the alarm did not sound."
>
> Explicit cause, if established: "The alarm did not sound because the battery was empty."

Do not infer causality merely to improve syntax.

### Combine related actions without adding detail

Combine short sentences when they describe one continuous movement and separate emphasis is unnecessary.

> Repetitive: "She went to the market. She bought some vegetables. She returned home."
>
> Connected: "She went to the market, bought some vegetables, and returned home."

Do not add qualities such as "fresh" or change the action merely to make the revision more vivid.

### State the claim hidden in a trailing phrase

Phrases beginning with "highlighting," "ensuring," "reflecting," "showcasing," or "fostering" often append an unsupported benefit or interpretation.

> Empty addition: "The service stores the file, ensuring a seamless experience."
>
> Direct: "The service stores the file."

Where a trailing phrase contains a substantive consequence, state that consequence clearly and support the relationship. Keep useful participial constructions whose actor and meaning are clear.

### Keep modifiers beside what they modify

Place "only," "not," "almost," and time expressions next to the relevant word or phrase. "Only the client retries" and "The client only retries" make different claims.

Give introductory participial phrases a clear subject. "Walking to the station, the rain started" accidentally gives the rain the action of walking. Write "Rain started while I was walking to the station."

### Give pronouns clear referents

"It," "they," "this," and "which" should point to a recognizable noun or proposition. Where two referents are plausible, repeat the noun. In technical prose, name the referent rather than asking "this" or "which" to stand for an entire preceding clause.

> Ambiguous: "When the client contacted the server, it restarted."
>
> Clear: "The server restarted when the client contacted it."

### Keep the small words that carry grammar

Retain articles, verbs, and conjunctions when they clarify the relationship. "Remove the backup file" is clearer than "Remove backup file."

Repeat an article when a series could otherwise describe one object. "The client and the host" clearly names two entities. Preserve "that" when it helps the reader recognize a clause, as in "Ensure that the switch is off."

Complete a parallel construction with its necessary verbs. "Phase 1 moves the converters, and Phase 2 moves the runtime" avoids making the reader reconstruct a missing action.

### Avoid noun piles and accidental ambiguity

Break long strings of nouns into relationships.

> Dense: "The proto import budget check script."
>
> Clear: "The script that checks the proto-import budget."

Keep parallel items grammatically parallel. Clarify the grouping of "and" and "or" with "both," "either," or a separate sentence when the grouping affects meaning.

### Use grammatically complete prose by default

Avoid dropped articles, verbless fragments, arrows, and compressed notes in ordinary explanation. "Parser rejects bad date → exit 2, no write" becomes "The parser rejects a bad date, exits with code 2, and writes nothing." Use complete sentences with enough structure to be understood on first reading.

Fragments can be necessary in labels, quoted speech, dialogue, or a specific literary effect. The style exception does not authorize a whole explanation made of rhetorical fragments. Intentional character speech differs from accidental grammatical failure.

### Preserve tense, agreement, and reference

Maintain subject-verb agreement, intelligible tense relationships, consistent person, and unambiguous possessives. A change of tense should correspond to a change of time or narrative perspective.

Correct errors without flattening established dialect or quoted language. A grammatical change that alters a character's speech or a source's wording requires a different treatment from correcting the surrounding narration.

## Vocabulary and expression

### Prefer the familiar word that says the right thing

Use "use" rather than "utilize" or "leverage," "help" rather than "facilitate," "many" rather than "numerous," and "if" rather than "in the event that." Prefer "do" to "perform" when they mean the same thing.

A less common word is justified when it supplies a necessary distinction. Precision can require a technical term, a historically appropriate word, or a particular literary image. Rarity itself is not a virtue.

### Replace inflated wording with the actual meaning

Repeatedly reaching for elevated language makes unrelated subjects sound alike. Replace the vague use with a plain term, the relevant action, or the supported relationship.

| Stock use | More direct treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Additionally | Remove it unless the addition needs marking; otherwise use "also." |
| crucial, pivotal | State what depends on it or what changes because of it. |
| delve | Use "examine," "read," or the actual action. |
| enduring | State the duration or omit the unsupported claim. |
| enhance | State what improves and how. |
| fostering | Name the action and the supported result. |
| garner | Use "receive," "gain," or the particular action. |
| interplay | Describe how the factors affect one another. |
| intricate | Explain the relevant complexity. |
| landscape | Name the field, market, environment, or situation. |
| showcase | Use "show" or state what the example demonstrates. |
| tapestry | Name the elements and their relationship. |
| testament | State the evidence and its limited implication. |
| underscore | State the point directly. |
| vibrant | Name the visible qualities or observable activity. |

Literal and necessary technical meanings remain valid. A woven tapestry, a painted landscape, and a mathematical operation do not become vague merely because the words also appear in weak prose.

### Say "is" or "has" when that is the relationship

Replace "serves as," "stands as," "boasts," and "features" when they merely decorate identification or possession. Keep a verb that describes a different relationship, such as a room serving two purposes.

### Remove filler without removing meaning

| Padded expression | Direct expression |
| --- | --- |
| In order to | To |
| Due to the fact that | Because |
| In the event that | If |
| It is important to note that | State the relevant fact. |
| Could potentially possibly | Use the single qualification the claim needs. |
| Actually, very, basically | Remove unless the word adds a real correction, degree, or limitation. |

Do not replace filler with a stronger unsupported judgment. "This is actually a very good point" does not become more informative as "This is an excellent point." Explain the point's relevance where that explanation is needed.

### Keep terminology stable

Call the same entity by the same name. Repeating an accurate noun is clearer than calling one character "the protagonist," "the main character," "the central figure," and "the hero" in the same paragraph.

Vary sentence construction when prose becomes repetitive. Do not disguise repetition by substituting approximate synonyms. "Start" and "initiate" should not appear to describe different actions when they describe the same action.

### Choose verbs that preserve the actual action

Prefer a precise verb to a weak verb supported by a modifier. "Shuffled" can replace "walked slowly" when the gait really is a shuffle. "Snapped" can replace "said angrily" when the speech is abrupt.

Do not intensify or change an action to remove an adverb. "Walked slowly" may be the most accurate wording. "Whispered" does not mean "said quietly" in every context, and "sprinted" adds information that "moved quickly" may not supply.

Keep adverbs that determine scope, frequency, degree, sequence, or uncertainty. "Usually fails" and "fails" are different claims.

### Prefer direct verbs to abstract constructions

"The team decided" usually expresses "The team made a decision" more directly. "The service stores the file" is clearer than "The service performs file storage."

An abstract noun is appropriate when the concept itself is the subject. Do not replace every nominalization indiscriminately. "Authentication failed" may be the exact established event.

### Use literal meaning and purposeful imagery

In explanatory prose, name the operation, relation, responsibility, or goal. "The database stays close at hand" and "types that follow your schema" do not identify a useful mechanism. Technical-sounding abstractions can make an ordinary idea harder to understand.

In storytelling, a concrete image can create an experience that literal summary cannot. It must fit the character, world, and moment. Reusable imagery such as "a tapestry of emotions" remains generic.

| Term or construction | Plain treatment |
| --- | --- |
| substrate | "Base," or the actual material or system. |
| wedge; wedge in | Name the entry point; use "add" for the action. |
| vector | "Way" or "method" when the mathematical or technical meaning is absent. |
| locus | Name the location, responsibility, or subject. |
| vantage | Name the perspective and whose it is. |
| nexus | Describe the connection. |
| primitive as an abstract noun | Name the operation, type, or building block precisely. |
| harness as a metaphor | Name the framework, program, or equipment. |
| surface, as in "API surface" | Name the exposed operations, methods, or interface. |
| bedrock | Name the basis or requirement. |
| scaffolding as a metaphor | Name the setup, supporting code, or temporary structure. |
| modality | Name the format, channel, sense, or method. |
| paradigm | Name the approach or model. |
| gold-plating | "More than the job needs," or the particular unnecessary addition. |
| ratchet as a metaphor | Name the mechanism, or say "a limit that only tightens." |
| evacuate for moving code | "Move out." |
| endgame | "The last phase," or its actual task. |
| north star | Name the goal or decision criterion. |
| flywheel | Explain the repeated process and its reinforcing effect. |

An established technical term may be necessary in its actual sense. Explain it when the audience needs help. Do not replace one vague metaphor with another.

Remove aphorisms, rhetorical fragments, personified code, and figurative verbs where a literal statement would be clearer. "A dial worth turning" can become "a parameter worth varying." Replace "rides along," "stands on," and "the plan holds it" with the actual relationship.

An image needs a particular expressive purpose. A phrase such as "wire it or delete it" may sound decisive while concealing the actual requirement.

### Do not assign unsupported intentions

Describe an animal's behavior, a system's operation, or a process's effect without inventing thoughts or motives. A dog spreading scent is an observation or hypothesis to support; the dog declaring "This is mine!" is an imagined interpretation.

Necessary technical shorthand such as "the server rejects the request" names behavior. Claims that a product "understands what you need" require a more precise account. Literary personification has its place in storytelling when it expresses a particular perception.

### Express enthusiasm proportionately

Let the material establish interest. Avoid multiplying superlatives, exclamation marks, flattering introductions, and invitations to marvel together. A measured tone can still express curiosity, pleasure, grief, anger, or conviction.

Do not replace "very good" with "genuinely impressive" and assume the sentence now contains evidence. Either judgment needs an appropriate purpose or basis.

## Rhythm and coherence

### Vary sentence movement for meaning

Short sentences can establish a point or mark a turn. Longer sentences can carry a related condition, explanation, or sustained perception. Choose their lengths according to the thought, not an alternating numerical pattern.

Vary clauses and openings when repetition becomes monotonous. Keep repetition that supplies a necessary reference or a specific literary effect. Do not insert unusual syntax merely to make the text less predictable.

### Avoid both monotony and artificial variety

Several sentences beginning with "I think" may obscure the distinctions among the claims. Removing the repeated phrase can help. Replacing it with a succession of equally empty introductions does not.

> Mechanical: "I think the idea is useful. I think we should try it. I think it will work."
>
> More direct: "The idea seems useful. We should try it, and I expect it to work."

The writer's degree of confidence stays intact. Varying the phrasing is not permission to strengthen or weaken the claim.

### Let paragraph length follow the material

Give a difficult explanation enough space. Give an important moment enough development. Let a short paragraph emphasize a genuine turn rather than making every sentence look important.

Uniform paragraph lengths are not a quality standard. Neither are constant variations. Paragraph breaks should follow shifts in thought, emphasis, speaker, time, or scene.

## Punctuation and presentation

### Use periods and commas for ordinary separation

The default house style excludes em dashes, en dashes used as sentence punctuation, hyphens used as substitute dashes, and parenthetical punctuation. Give a separate thought its own sentence or integrate the information grammatically with commas.

Do not create a comma splice while removing a dash. Two independent sentences cannot ordinarily be joined by a comma alone. Use a period or a suitable conjunction.

Use periods rather than semicolons in ordinary prose. Do not replace a prohibited dash with a semicolon merely to retain the same interrupted sentence.

Preserve meaningful lexical hyphens, exact identifiers, ranges or notation required by a formal convention, and punctuation inside faithful quotations. Academic citations and necessary mathematical or technical notation have the narrow exceptions explained in the style sections.

### Reserve colons for lists and examples

Use a colon after a complete introduction to a list or example. Avoid using it as a habitual bridge between two thoughts, after an introductory condition, or after a redundant bold label.

> Weak: "If you are changing the settings: restart the service."
>
> Clear: "Restart the service after changing the settings."

A colon inside an exact command, identifier, citation, or required notation is part of that material.

### Use straight quotation marks

Use straight single and double quotes in authored prose. Preserve apostrophes in contractions and possessives. Do not change the punctuation of faithfully reproduced source quotations or required publication notation merely to normalize typography.

### Use sentence-case headings

Capitalize the first word and proper names. Make a heading informative. "When the backup expires" supplies more direction than "Important considerations."

Keep a logical heading hierarchy. Use headings where they help navigation, rather than as decorations before every short paragraph.

### Use emphasis sparingly

Bold text should help readers locate an important distinction or a necessary interface label. Do not bold every proper noun, acronym, sentence opening, or repeated term.

Avoid a bold label followed by a colon and a restatement. A compact label followed by new information can be useful.

> Redundant: "**Performance:** Performance improved."
>
> Useful structure: "**Schema in TypeScript.** Tables live in one file."

The example illustrates a presentation form. Its factual statement must be true of the subject being described.

### Let lists express actual structure

Use numbered lists for ordered sequences. Use bullets for parallel, unordered items. Introduce a list with a complete sentence and keep its grammar parallel.

Use paragraphs for connected prose. A list of disconnected sentence fragments cannot replace an explanation of the relationship among them. Tables suit comparisons or mappings, not decoration.

Do not force three items into a list, three clauses into a sentence, or three examples into a paragraph when the material naturally contains a different number.

### Remove decorative interface and chatbot residue

Omit decorative emojis from headings and bullets. Remove canned acknowledgments, artificial congratulations, invitations to continue, and generic sign-offs from the substantive prose.

Examples include "I hope this helps!," "Let me know if...," "Of course!," "Certainly!," "Found the smoking gun!," and automatic flattery such as "Great question!" or "You're absolutely right!" Begin with the useful information.

Exact quotations, discussion of emoji usage, and content whose subject requires such symbols are separate cases. A symbol used as data is not decoration.

## Style-specific conventions

The general guidelines apply to every style. The following sections add the conventions necessary to each form. An exception needs a particular function; describing it as intentional is insufficient.

| Style | Purpose | Characteristic treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Academic | Establish a scholarly claim and its evidential limits. | Formal, precise, qualified, and explicit about the relationship between evidence and interpretation. |
| Technical | Enable action, lookup, or understanding of a system. | Literal, consistent, and organized around the document's practical purpose. |
| Absolute | Convey the useful answer with minimal reading effort. | Plain, direct, concise, and grammatically complete. |
| Article | Develop an informative or argued account. | Concrete openings, coherent development, relevant evidence, and purposeful sections. |
| Storytelling | Create a particular narrative experience. | Voice, viewpoint, scene, description, dialogue, and disclosure working together. |

Required citation notation, exact technical syntax, and character speech can depart from ordinary presentation defaults. Such exceptions do not excuse ornamental jargon, fabricated evidence, or generic expression.

### Academic

#### Formality with accessible language

Use formal English and familiar words where they express the relevant concept accurately. Avoid slang, conversational fillers, forced intimacy, and promotional language. Formality comes from precision, disciplined claims, and explicit reasoning.

> Inflated: "The present investigation endeavors to elucidate the intricate interplay between the aforementioned factors."
>
> Direct: "This study examines how the factors interact."

Name the factors where the surrounding text does not already identify them. Retain a term such as "interaction" when it names a defined statistical relationship, rather than replacing it with a looser everyday paraphrase.

#### Disciplinary voice and researcher position

Use first person when it identifies an argument, a research decision, or a relevant relationship to the material. "I interpret the passage as a challenge to that account" identifies the interpretive claim as the writer's. First person is not a requirement for natural academic prose, and third person is not a guarantee of objectivity.

| Kind of writing | Relevant emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| Empirical research | Distinguish measured observations, reported experiences, analytical decisions, and interpretations. Make the researcher's agency clear where it affects the evidence. |
| Interpretive scholarship | Ground an interpretation in the text, object, record, or experience under discussion. Close reading and contextual description should support the interpretive move. |
| Theoretical argument | Define concepts, expose assumptions, explain relationships, and distinguish proposed propositions from established findings. |
| Practitioner-facing research | Explain the practical consequence, intended users, conditions, and constraints without promising outcomes beyond the evidence. |

Match the particular field, form, and venue rather than assigning one voice to all work in a discipline. A method can use passive voice to foreground a specimen while a discussion uses active voice to identify an argument. Humanities scholarship may discuss an enduring text in the present tense while describing its publication history in the past.

Where positionality affects interpretation, describe the relevant relationship concretely. State what access, involvement, institutional role, or experience shaped the work. A generic claim to neutrality or a personal confession does not explain that relationship. Keep participant perspectives distinguishable from the researcher's interpretation of them.

#### Research questions and contributions

State the question precisely enough to identify the phenomenon, population or material, setting, and relationship that matter. Distinguish a descriptive question from a comparative, predictive, causal, or exploratory one. The purpose of the paper should answer the problem established in its introduction.

Describe the contribution as a specific change in understanding, evidence, method, or interpretation. Explain the relationship to the nearest relevant prior work instead of declaring the entire topic unexplored. A gap concerning a mechanism is not answered merely by another estimate of prevalence.

Bound claims such as "the first study" or "no previous research" by what the available literature and documented search can support. "To our knowledge" alone does not explain the scope of that knowledge. Where an absence claim matters, identify the relevant search scope and date. Prefer an accurate statement of the remaining uncertainty when a stronger novelty claim cannot be supported.

> Inflated: "No research has examined this problem."
>
> More specific form: "The reviewed studies estimate prevalence in urban schools. Evidence about the mechanism in rural schools remains limited."

The second version requires both statements to match the reviewed literature. A limitation of the available review is not necessarily a gap in the field as a whole.

#### Titles, abstracts, and keywords

A title should identify the central subject and the scope needed to distinguish the paper. Put useful concepts ahead of empty openers such as "An investigation of." Include the population, setting, material, or design when that information changes what readers should expect. Avoid unfamiliar abbreviations and wordplay whose meaning remains obscure.

A declarative title makes a substantive claim and needs the same evidential restraint as the paper. A question title should identify a real question the work addresses. Neither brevity nor a striking phrase justifies omitting a condition that changes the meaning.

An abstract should stand alone and accurately represent the paper's purpose, approach, principal findings, and contribution. Include decisive sample or contextual information and qualifications within the available space. Report actual findings rather than announcing that results or implications will be discussed. Do not add a result, causal claim, or recommendation absent from the body.

Structured and unstructured abstracts serve different publication forms. Follow the required form and length without importing a universal word count or a fixed number of sentences. Compression should remove background and repetition before it removes a qualification that determines whether a claim is true.

Keywords should identify the paper's actual concepts, population, setting, or distinctive method using terms relevant readers would search for. Avoid generic entries such as "study" or "research" unless they are themselves part of the subject. Keep terminology aligned with the title, abstract, and main text.

#### Literature synthesis

Organize a literature review around the questions, theoretical positions, findings, methods, or disagreements relevant to the argument. Chronology is useful when development over time matters. An author-by-author inventory is insufficient when the reader needs an account of how the studies relate.

State the claim each source contributes. Explain where findings converge, conflict, or depend on different measurements, populations, settings, or designs. Distinguish a scholar's argument from an empirical result. Group citations when they support the same proposition, and separate them when they support materially different ones.

> Inventory: "One study examined attendance. Another study examined attendance. A third study discussed attendance."
>
> Synthesis form: "The studies agree that attendance declined, but they measure different periods. Only the longitudinal study follows the same pupils over time."

The synthesis is suitable only if those relationships are established. Do not invent agreement, two opposing camps, or a common mechanism merely to make the review read as an argument.

Explain why a reported gap matters to the question. Limited coverage of a location or population does not automatically make another study valuable. Keep foundational work where it establishes conceptual origins, and use sufficiently current evidence for claims that depend on recent developments. Neither age nor prestige alone determines a source's relevance.

#### Sections that perform distinct functions

Let the article type and its reasoning determine the structure. These functions can occupy separate sections or be combined where the form requires it.

| Section | Writing function |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction | Establish the specific problem, necessary context, relevant uncertainty, and purpose. Make the question and contribution recognizable without an unnecessarily broad historical opening. |
| Literature or conceptual background | Establish the knowledge and concepts the argument depends on. Synthesize relevant evidence and explain the unresolved issue. |
| Methods | Describe the design, material or participants, measures, procedures, and analysis in enough detail to understand how the evidence was produced. Explain consequential choices and limits. |
| Results or findings | Present the findings relevant to the questions, including material negative or inconclusive findings. Lead with what was found rather than an unexplained name of a test. |
| Discussion | Explain what the findings mean, how they relate to prior work, what alternatives remain, and what the limitations permit. Distinguish supported implications from proposals for further investigation. |
| Conclusion | State the contribution the paper has established and its essential bounds. Avoid new evidence, a stronger version of the finding, or a generic demand for more research. |

When results and discussion are separate, keep the distinction between reporting evidence and explaining its implications visible. Qualitative and interpretive work may integrate evidence and analysis more closely. A conventional section label does not require concealing the interpretation necessary to understand a finding.

| Form | Additional structural emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| Empirical paper | Connect the question, design, findings, and interpretation. Explain the extent to which the design supports the conclusion. |
| Research review | State scope and selection principles sufficiently to explain what the synthesis covers. Distinguish systematic coverage from a selective or interpretive account. |
| Theoretical paper | Establish the problem in the existing account, develop the reasoning, and specify what the proposed extension explains or leaves open. |
| Case study | Supply the case's context, selection rationale, evidence, and analytical relevance. Explain possible transfer to other settings without assuming population-wide representativeness. |
| Research proposal | Distinguish existing evidence and completed work from planned methods, expected outcomes, and potential benefits. Connect objectives to a feasible approach and acknowledge consequential uncertainties. |
| Policy brief | Make the problem, evidence, options, tradeoffs, and recommendation accessible to the intended decision-maker. Distinguish a supported finding from a value judgment or preferred action, and state implementation constraints relevant to the recommendation. |

These are available forms, not mandatory outlines. Use only the sections and amount of signposting that the argument and publication require.

#### Methods and findings in concrete terms

Describe what was actually done and observed. Specify relevant selection, recruitment, material, timing, instruments, exclusions, missing data, and analytical choices without filling unknown details with plausible conventions. Use "randomly assigned" only for actual random assignment. Do not turn an intention into a completed procedure or an observed practice into a formally approved one.

Define a construct through the measure or evidence used to represent it where that distinction matters. A self-report scale of confidence does not directly measure competence, and an interview account of behavior differs from observation of that behavior. Name the distinction without dismissing a method that suits the question.

Distinguish prior hypotheses from explanations developed after the findings were known. Describe exploratory analyses as exploratory. An unexpected result can be informative without being rewritten as a prediction the study made in advance.

Present qualitative findings through an intelligible relationship between the interpretation and its supporting material. Attribute participants' words accurately, explain relevant context, and preserve the meaning of translated terms. A quotation can illustrate a theme without establishing its prevalence. Counts of coded interviews need their actual denominator and do not automatically describe a wider population.

#### Statistical findings with substantive meaning

Report important estimates with the sample information, magnitude, units, and uncertainty needed to interpret them. Where relevant to the method and venue, identify the statistic, degrees of freedom, exact probability value, effect measure, and interval. Explain a result in the terms of the research question rather than leaving a string of symbols to carry the conclusion.

Distinguish standard deviation, standard error, and an interval for an estimate. They describe different quantities and should retain their proper labels. Identify adjusted estimates, comparison groups, and relevant differences between the original sample and the sample used in a particular analysis.

Describe multiplicity adjustments, sensitivity analyses, and consequential departures from the stated analysis when they affect interpretation. Report what those analyses establish without calling a finding "robust" solely because one alternative specification produces a similar result.

Include relevant negative, contradictory, and inconclusive findings. Do not hide them by changing the outcome of interest, omitting an inconvenient subgroup, or presenting an exploratory pattern as the sole result. Keep numbers, comparison directions, labels, and qualifications consistent across prose, tables, figures, titles, and abstracts.

Use tables and figures for detail that benefits from comparison, and use prose to explain the principal finding and its meaning. Do not repeat every cell. Captions and notes should identify the sample, measures, units, abbreviations, uncertainty, and source information needed to understand the display.

#### Claims, evidence, and limits

Distinguish the research question, method, observation, interpretation, and limitation. State what the evidence supports without making its implications universal.

> Too strong: "The survey proves that remote work improves productivity."
>
> Bounded form: "In this survey, remote workers reported higher productivity."

The bounded sentence is appropriate only if those are the actual findings. Self-reported productivity differs from measured productivity, and an association differs from a causal effect.

Place qualifications where they affect the claim. Avoid strings of "perhaps," "possibly," "arguably," and "it could be suggested." One accurate qualification is usually stronger than several evasive ones.

#### Substantive sentences and stable terminology

Avoid trivial observations that merely announce the importance of the topic. Each sentence should establish a necessary fact, define a concept, explain a relation, supply evidence, or delimit the argument.

Use a consistent name for a construct, measure, group, or method. Reduce unnecessary repeated phrasing without substituting a near-synonym for a defined term.

> Repetitive: "The findings are important. These important findings have important implications."
>
> Informative form: State the particular implication and the evidence supporting it.

#### Syntax that makes reasoning explicit

Keep the main proposition visible. Use subordinate clauses to express actual dependencies, conditions, and concessions. Split long sentences whose claims need separate development.

Active voice can identify responsibility: "We excluded incomplete responses." Passive voice can foreground the method: "Incomplete responses were excluded." Neither construction determines scholarly legitimacy.

Use tense according to the relationship being described. Completed procedures and observations generally concern the past; established definitions and the current argument may use the present. Avoid tense shifts that obscure whether a statement reports a result or asserts a continuing fact.

#### Necessary formal conventions

Citation systems may require parentheses, brackets, abbreviations, and punctuation excluded from ordinary house prose. Preserve the required notation. Equations, units, variable names, and technical labels also retain their exact form.

These exceptions cover scholarly function. They do not permit stacks of parenthetical commentary, needless Latin expressions, or more elaborate words chosen merely to sound academic.

Use the citation system required by the publication consistently. An author-date system and a numbered system organize references differently. Neither is a universal default for scholarly English. Keep citations connected to their reference entries, and preserve the exact bibliographic details needed to identify the work. Required quotation locators, reference punctuation, title capitalization, statistical notation, and table conventions take precedence over ordinary presentation defaults within their formal scope.

#### Correspondence and declarations

Academic correspondence should identify the substantive point directly and use a professional tone. A submission letter can explain the paper's question, contribution, and relevance to the intended readership without praising the journal or overstating novelty.

A response to criticism should accurately identify the issue, explain the change or reasoned disagreement, and locate the relevant passage where useful. Separate completed changes from proposed work. Support disagreements with reasoning and evidence. Formulaic gratitude does not answer a methodological concern, and a respectful tone does not require accepting an unsupported criticism.

State author contributions, funding, conflicts, ethics approval, consent, assistance, and data or code availability accurately when such declarations are required. Describe the specific contribution or activity rather than relying on a vague assurance. Do not claim approval, access, preregistration, independent review, or verification that did not occur.

An availability statement should distinguish openly accessible material, restricted access, access on request, and material that cannot be shared. Give the real access conditions. Describe relevant assistance by its actual use and extent, with the detail the applicable publication requires. Do not use a declaration to imply that another person or a tool assumes responsibility for the paper's claims.

#### Worked example

> Before: "It is important to note that the results clearly demonstrate that all participants benefited from the intervention, potentially suggesting a revolutionary development."
>
> After, if the evidence supports the narrower claim: "Participants reported benefits after the intervention. The study does not establish whether the intervention caused those changes."

The revision illustrates bounded reporting. It requires evidence for both the reported benefits and the methodological limitation. Caution does not justify adding a limitation or result that the evidence does not establish.

### Technical

#### Use controlled English with practical flexibility

Use a house style inspired by ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (STE). Favor direct commands, active voice, simple sentence structures, and stable terminology. Apply these principles to documentation and reusable agent instructions.

Treat STE as a strong starting point. This house style does not require its approved-word dictionary, every grammatical restriction, or strict sentence limits. Use a technical term, longer sentence, or explanatory passage when it communicates the task more accurately. Keep each departure tied to a concrete benefit, such as preserving a necessary condition or explaining a difficult relationship. Do not claim STE compliance or assign a compliance percentage.

Reduce reading effort and unnecessary tokens while preserving the information needed for correct execution. Shorter text alone does not establish lower total task cost or better results.

#### Organize around the reader's purpose

A technical document should support one main purpose. Keep substantial material serving another purpose in a separately identified section or document, connected by a descriptive link.

| Form | Reader's need | Writing requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tutorial | Learn through a successful practical experience. | State what the reader will build. Provide a supported sequence, visible results, expected outputs, and concrete progress. Keep background explanation brief. |
| How-to | Complete a particular task. | Assume relevant competence. Describe necessary actions and choices. Name the guide after the task and keep unrelated background out of the sequence. |
| Reference | Find dependable facts. | State options, behavior, constraints, and errors. Mirror the structure of the system and put information where readers expect it. Avoid persuasion and tutorial narration. |
| Explanation | Understand a bounded subject and its reasons. | Explain the relevant why, design decisions, context, history, alternatives, and tradeoffs. Keep the account readable apart from a task sequence. |

Tutorial language can use an inclusive "we" for a shared learning sequence. Instructions generally address "you" or use commands. Reference prose describes the system. Explanation can express a reasoned judgment about design tradeoffs.

A how-to title identifies the task, such as "How to calibrate the radar array." An explanation title names a bounded subject that could follow "About." A reference gives definite facts while retaining conditions that genuinely affect behavior.

#### Make instructions actionable

Write direct commands: "Click Submit" or "Install the component." Use active voice and give each instruction one principal action. Name the actor when several people, agents, or systems could perform it. Name the object instead of using an ambiguous "it" or "this."

Use numbered steps when order matters. Keep prerequisites, actions, and verification in execution order. State the expected result and observable completion condition where readers need them to recognize success.

Put conditions, warnings, and prerequisites before the actions they govern. State the common case first and exceptions afterward, unless an exception must be known before a consequential action.

Describe the visible result where it helps readers recognize progress. A tutorial benefits from the expected output, changed prompt, or relevant log line.

Use a knowledgeable, approachable tone without adding "please" to instructions. Avoid "simply," "easy," and "quickly" when they minimize the reader's effort. State a supported duration separately where it matters.

Use the present tense for current behavior. Reserve "will" for an actual future event. Do not insert promises about planned features into descriptions of current capability.

#### Distinguish requirements, defaults, and options

Use ordinary commands for workflow steps. Reserve "must" and "must not" for critical requirements and prohibitions. Use "default to" for a preferred choice that permits a justified alternative, and "may" for an optional action. Use "can" to describe capability. Make permission and uncertainty distinguishable from context.

State the condition for an exception. Avoid "always" and "never" unless the rule applies throughout the stated scope. These are house conventions, not a claim of ISO requirements-language compliance.

> "The export must preserve record IDs. Default to CSV. For nested records, you may use JSON."

#### Keep terminology and syntax dependable

Use the actual symbol, file, flag, command, and operation names. Keep one term for each operation and one meaning for each term. If "check" means inspect, do not also use it to mean restrain.

Explain necessary technical terms near their first relevant use. Avoid idioms, colloquialisms, unnecessary Latin abbreviations, and figurative descriptions that make interpretation or translation harder.

Prefer familiar words and simple clauses. Keep articles, verbs, and connecting words that make the relationship explicit. Avoid compressed fragments, noun piles, and synonym changes made only for variety.

An instruction longer than about 20 words, or another sentence longer than about 25, often benefits from simplification. These are practical density signals rather than fixed limits. Keep a longer sentence when it carries one clear thought with its necessary condition.

#### Preserve exact notation

Write "a, b, or both" when that is the meaning of "a/b" or "and/or." Preserve slashes, parentheses, colons, and other syntax inside exact paths, commands, units, and formal notation.

Use explicit singular or plural forms rather than "file(s)." Move ordinary parenthetical explanations into grammatical clauses or separate sentences.

Present code, commands, flags, and paths in code formatting. Use bold where readers need to identify a UI label. Indent snippets with tabs where the language permits them, preserving indentation that determines meaning.

Use real names and paths in factual documentation. Declare illustrative values as examples. Counts and directory trees must correspond to the described state; include the command that reproduces a derived count or tree when it matters.

#### Make documents easy to navigate

Use task headings as verb phrases, such as "Create an instance," and concept headings as noun phrases. Keep one main heading and a logical hierarchy.

Use descriptive link text rather than "click here." Include essential context on the current page rather than making the reader follow a link for the basic explanation.

For a procedure or reusable agent instruction, state its purpose, relevant system context, inputs, constraints, and completion criteria. Use clear sections to separate these from the steps. Include surrounding behavior and reasons when they affect a decision. Remove repeated rules, filler, and background the intended reader already knows. Keep qualifications and explanations that prevent an incorrect action.

Use serial commas. Avoid "etc." when it conceals an unspecified category, and state when a list is partial.

#### Keep change descriptions focused

A PR description or commit message should identify the concrete problem and resulting behavior. Include the condition that exposes the problem and the evidence needed to assess the change.

Link extensive logs, commit lists, and large measurement tables. They should not replace the explanation.

Product interface text follows the needs of its screen and action. Documentation conventions do not justify long explanations inside a button, notification, or field hint.

#### Example

> Dense: "Configuration of the proto import ratchet budget script parameters is performed via budget.json. Note that it's important to remember that running with --write, which updates the committed budget to reflect the current count, should only be done when lowering it. If exceeded, CI fails."
>
> Clear: "The script reads the import budget from `budget.json`. If the current count exceeds the budget, CI fails. Run the script with `--write` only to lower the budget."

The revision identifies the file, condition, and action while keeping the program's stated behavior intact.

> Overcompressed: "Schema valid → write; else stop."
>
> Clear: "Validate the generated JSON against the schema. If validation passes, save the JSON to the requested path. Otherwise, report the validation errors."

The clear version names the object, condition, destination, and failure response. Removing those details would make the instruction shorter but less dependable.

> Justified longer instruction: "If the migration changes a public field name and existing clients still use that field, preserve the old name as an alias until those clients have migrated."

This instruction exceeds the STE procedural sentence limit. Keep the combined condition and timing because they determine when the compatibility action applies. Splitting or simplifying is useful only if those relationships remain clear.

### Absolute

#### Directness without lost meaning

State the useful answer in plain language. Keep the reason, condition, consequence, or necessary limitation close to it. Remove jargon that the reader does not need, and explain necessary terminology through its actual effect.

Keep the tone neutral and coherent. Directness does not require brusqueness, hostility, certainty, or telegraphic fragments.

#### Complete sentences with no padding

Omit greetings, pre-narration, decorative headings, repeated summaries, flattering acknowledgments, and invitations to continue. Keep articles and verbs. A short sentence that requires decoding is not concise in practice.

> Compressed: "Token absent → auth fail; retry useless."
>
> Clear: "The request has no token. Add one before retrying."

Use the second form only if the missing token is established as the cause. A terse answer must preserve the distinction between an observed cause and a possibility.

#### Necessary explanation stays

Keep a qualification that changes the answer. Keep a warning that affects an action. Keep the distinction between a recommendation and a requirement.

> Unhelpfully terse: "Delete it."
>
> Sufficient: "Delete the duplicate after confirming that the original opens."

The condition is part of the recommendation, not removable padding.

#### Worked example

> Before: "Certainly! At a high level, the underlying issue appears to be an authentication-related impediment that could potentially be addressed through token renewal."
>
> After: "The token may have expired. Renew it and retry."

The revision keeps the uncertainty. If expiry is established, "The token expired" is appropriate. If renewal would create a risk or requires a different procedure, the necessary condition belongs in the answer.

### Article

#### Concrete material and a reason to read

Lead with an artifact, observation, example, event, supported number, or specific tension. Let it establish why the subject matters. Explain the general point after the concrete material where that sequence helps understanding.

Avoid broad introductions about an evolving world, introductory biography that does not serve the argument, and announcements that the piece will explore its topic.

#### Headlines, standfirsts, and news leads

A headline should identify the story accurately at the level of certainty the reporting supports. Preserve consequential limits of population, timeframe, and evidence. A question headline must not insinuate an allegation the article cannot substantiate. Search-friendly wording and brevity do not justify a stronger claim than the story establishes.

A standfirst, the short summary beneath a headline, should supply the development, context, or stakes that help readers understand the article. Add useful information rather than restating the headline in synonyms. Keep the same factual scope across the headline, standfirst, and body.

A news lead should establish the consequential development and the essential people, place, or timing. Include the facts needed to understand the event without forcing every available detail into the first sentence. Prefer the decision or consequence to a routine announcement that a meeting occurred.

A feature may begin with a scene or an individual experience. Give readers a timely explanation of the broader subject and stakes so they can understand why the opening matters. A delayed explanation should create purposeful interest rather than conceal a weak premise.

> Meeting-centered: "The council held a meeting on Tuesday to discuss fares."
>
> Outcome-centered, if established: "The council voted on Tuesday to increase bus fares from $2 to $2.50 in January."

The outcome-centered version requires the vote, amounts, and effective date to be known. Where a month could refer to more than one year, include the year. Do not turn a discussion or proposal into an adopted decision to strengthen a lead.

#### A coherent line of development

Give each section one main contribution. Develop the argument through evidence, examples, implications, and relevant objections. Do not manufacture a symmetrical outline or insert headings merely to make a long piece look organized.

Keep the author's actual position recognizable. A distinctive article can be restrained, expansive, skeptical, reflective, or conversational. A generic personality should not replace the material's own perspective.

Tight sentences are the default, but a sustained thought may need a longer sentence or paragraph. Expansion should add explanation or experience rather than another paraphrase of the thesis.

#### Credibility and proportion

Use proof rather than promotional adjectives. Support factual claims with the available sources. Preserve the distinction among a reported case, an illustrative example, and an opinion.

Do not invent customer experiences, credentials, results, conversations, or personal vulnerability. Avoid an autobiographical arc added solely to create emotional credibility.

Attribute allegations and disputed accounts clearly. Distinguish what records establish, what a source says, what the subject disputes, and what remains unanswered. Present an available response in a way that preserves its substance. Do not imply that a person declined to comment unless that is what occurred, or use an absence of comment as evidence of guilt.

#### Data reporting with context and human consequences

Lead with the finding that answers the reporting question, then give readers the comparisons needed to understand its scale and significance. Historical context, geographic comparisons, and trends should use comparable definitions and periods. Explain whether a reported change concerns the underlying phenomenon or the way it was recorded.

Connect a data pattern to its consequences for affected people where the evidence supports that connection. Individual accounts can show how a pattern is experienced. Their role and selection should be intelligible, especially when an unusual case attracts attention. Avoid presenting a dramatic anecdote as proof of how most people experience the issue.

Keep the relationship between data and testimony visible. Expert interpretation can explain a result but does not replace the finding or eliminate uncertainty. Differences between the records and people's accounts may be part of the story. Explain the discrepancy when possible and preserve it when unresolved.

Implications should follow from the established finding. Distinguish an observed trend from a forecast and a possible action from a demonstrated remedy. Do not end a data story with a confident prediction merely because the numbers form a striking pattern.

#### Public explanations of methodology

A methodology passage should let readers understand how the published finding was produced and where its limits lie. Identify the data publisher and source, relevant access or release date, coverage period, population, geographic scope, and definitions that affect the claim. Link to usable source material where available.

Explain the consequential analytical choices in plain language. Describe important exclusions, missing records, transformations, adjustments, and the basis for comparisons. State what an administrative record actually measures rather than treating it as a complete account of the underlying behavior. A record of reported incidents, for example, does not necessarily count every incident that occurred.

Describe material sensitivity to definitions or exclusions and any known limitations in coverage or accuracy. State what verification was actually performed without substituting a vague assurance that the data were checked. Explain whether readers can access the data, code, or supporting materials and identify genuine restrictions. A concise explanation in the article can link to fuller detail without hiding a limitation that changes the central finding.

#### Captions, chart text, and accessible descriptions

A caption should identify the relevant person, place, event, or evidence and supply necessary context. Preserve the distinction between the date an image was made and the event the article describes. Identify archival, illustrative, or reconstructed material when readers could otherwise mistake it for a record of the reported event. Supply credits where required.

Chart titles, labels, annotations, and notes should identify the quantity, units, period, population, and source needed to read the display. Distinguish counts from rates and adjusted from unadjusted values. A title or annotation should not assert a cause or conclusion the displayed evidence cannot support. Prose should not imply a scale, completeness, or precision the underlying data lack.

Alternative text should convey the image's relevant information or function in context. For a chart, describe the important relationship and essential uncertainty instead of offering only "a chart of the results." Provide an accessible data table or fuller text description when readers need detail that a short description cannot carry. Avoid redundant visual inventories and interpretations unsupported by the image or data.

#### Corrections and substantive updates

A correction should identify the material error and state the corrected information plainly. Where the error affected the central finding, explain the change to that finding. Do not obscure responsibility with "clarified" or "updated" when the earlier statement was wrong.

Distinguish a correction from new information about an evolving event. Give enough timing and context for readers to understand what changed. Do not repeat a harmful or misleading detail more extensively than needed to explain the correction.

#### Characteristic formulas to remove

Remove "In today's rapidly evolving landscape," "game-changer," "cutting-edge," "revolutionary," "here's why this matters" as an empty bridge, fake vulnerability arcs, biography padding, generic throat-clearing, and closing questions added only to increase engagement.

The underlying issue is unearned importance or fabricated connection. Replacing one formula with another preserves the problem.

#### Guides, essays, opinion, and newsletters

| Form | Writing requirements |
| --- | --- |
| Technical guide | Open with the reader's practical result. Use relevant commands, code, screenshots, or concrete output where available. End with an actionable consequence rather than a soft recap. |
| Essay | Develop one connected inquiry or argument. Begin with a specific observation, tension, or genuine contradiction. Let the progression earn the conclusion. |
| Opinion | Make the position explicit and answer to evidence. Represent relevant alternatives fairly rather than inventing weak opponents. |
| Newsletter | Make the opening screen useful. Keep diary material when it serves the piece. Use section labels only when they help readers navigate. |

An article can include narrative, but a reported anecdote must remain factual. Literary devices do not authorize invented reporting.

#### Worked example

> Before: "In today's rapidly evolving landscape, our revolutionary tool is a game-changer for busy teams. Here's why this matters."
>
> After, for an illustrative product with the stated behavior: "The tool saves each draft automatically, so a failed submission does not erase the team's work."

The revision begins with behavior and consequence. It is suitable only if both claims are true. If those facts are unknown, a concrete product explanation needs the actual established behavior.

### Storytelling

#### Particular experience rather than generic atmosphere

Make the scene belong to these characters, in this world, under these conditions. A viewpoint character notices details according to attention, knowledge, desire, fear, experience, and immediate purpose.

An inventory of everything a camera could see often contributes less than one detail that reveals what matters to the character. The appropriate detail may be visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, or conceptual. Do not add smells or bodily sensations to every scene merely to make it vivid.

> Generic: "The room was beautiful and full of memories."
>
> Illustrative scene: "Someone had polished around the pale rectangle where her father's photograph used to stand."

The second sentence invents a particular situation. It illustrates fictional specificity, not a factual paraphrase.

#### Voice, viewpoint, and distance

Keep the narrator's register and perspective intelligible. A change of viewpoint or narrative distance should have an identifiable function and enough orientation for the reader.

Do not give a character information they have not acquired. Distinguish the narrator's knowledge from the character's. In close viewpoint, filter descriptions and comparisons through what the character could notice and understand.

Vocabulary may reflect a character's background, occupation, relationships, and current emotional state. Avoid reducing those influences to caricature or using grammatical deviation as shorthand for intelligence.

#### Make narrative elements work together

Meaning, character, plot, disclosure, world, relationships, and prose influence one another:

| Concern | Writing implication |
| --- | --- |
| Meaning | Let actions, consequences, relationships, and contrasts carry the thematic question. |
| Character change | Make desires, beliefs, pressures, choices, and their effects intelligible where an arc depends on them. |
| Plot | Establish consequential relationships among events rather than a list of incidents. |
| Mystery | Control what the reader knows, suspects, misunderstands, and later reinterprets. |
| World | Make physical and social conditions constrain or enable what characters can do. |
| Relationships | Let bonds, status, obligations, and conflict create specific stakes. |
| Prose | Make sentence movement, detail, voice, and rhetorical choices serve the moment. |

A scene may affect several concerns at once. A setting detail can reveal a social rule and pressure a relationship. A line of dialogue can change an alliance and disclose a motive.

Let each scene contribute what the story needs. A flaw, crisis, and transformation can structure a character arc. Stasis, refusal, tragic failure, ensemble change, and a developing perception are also available forms.

#### Causality, cost, and consequence

Make choices matter where the story depends on agency. Establish the circumstances that make an action possible, its immediate effect, and the consequences that change later choices.

Coherence does not require an unbroken single-track plot or a protagonist who controls every outcome. Chance, institutions, other characters, and external events can matter. Avoid both arbitrary convenience and artificial tidiness.

World rules should remain consistent. A power, technology, or institution should not gain an unprepared capability solely to resolve the current difficulty. For speculative systems, reader understanding of a capability should support the credibility of its use in resolving conflict. Limits can create more interesting choices than additional powers. Develop established possibilities before adding new ones.

Maintain consistent physical details, geography, travel time, seasons, relationships, and knowledge. A character's changed behavior should have an intelligible basis where the story depends on that change.

#### Information, suspense, and revelation

Give readers enough orientation to understand the present scene while withholding information that creates a purposeful question. Distinguish productive uncertainty from confusion about who is acting, where the scene occurs, or what physically happened.

Mystery can concern an event, a motive, a relationship, a world rule, or a future outcome. Plant consequential details fairly. A revelation should change understanding where the story promises that effect.

Flashbacks, flash-forwards, delayed disclosure, and multiple timelines are available tools. Use them when their arrangement improves experience or interpretation. Temporal complexity is not a requirement, and linearity is not a defect.

An ending can resolve, partly resolve, or leave a question open. A continuing chapter may create forward interest without an artificial cliffhanger. A completed story may close its questions without becoming mechanically tidy.

Let choices and consequences carry the thematic meaning. The ending may retain moral ambiguity, acceptance, external action, or unresolved cost. Establish the outcome through the story rather than adding an explanatory lesson.

#### Emotion, behavior, and subtext

Use direct emotion labels, action, silence, dialogue, bodily sensation, or thought according to what the moment needs. "She was afraid" may be sufficient. A sequence of tightening chests, cold blood, caught breaths, and trembling hands can become as formulaic as an abstract label.

Emotion can appear through precise action:

- "She felt a surge of anger" becomes "She realized she'd been pressing her thumbnail into her palm hard enough to leave a crescent."
- "He was terrified" becomes "The key wouldn't fit because his hand was shaking too badly to align it with the lock."
- "She was sad about leaving" becomes "She straightened the cushions one more time, then closed the door without looking back."

Each alternative creates behavior that must belong to the character and scene. The first also introduces self-awareness and bodily pain. Use such alternatives as possible fictional choices, not interchangeable translations of emotion.

Avoid explaining subtext immediately after the reader can infer it. Characters may evade, conceal, misunderstand, or speak directly. Not every emotional exchange needs an unspoken second meaning.

#### Dialogue with identifiable speakers

Let vocabulary, syntax, rhythm, attention, and intention distinguish speakers. A line should belong to a particular person in a particular relationship, rather than to a generic witty narrator.

Dialogue can advance action, reveal character, establish a world, supply humor, or create disagreement. Avoid making every exchange a philosophical debate or a vehicle for the theme.

Dialogue tags should identify speech without drawing unnecessary attention. Character-specific action beats can reveal hesitation or intent. Replacing every "said" with an expressive verb or adverb creates noise.

Speaker identity should remain intelligible. Distinct voices help, but every line need not be identifiable without context or attribution. Retain the attribution readers need.

#### Pacing through syntax and proportion

Short clauses can create urgency. A longer sentence can sustain movement, thought, or pressure. Neither length automatically determines pace.

Give significant emotional consequences enough room. Keep transitions proportionate to their narrative importance. Remove repeated action, redundant dialogue, and description that delays a scene without changing its effect.

Prepare emotional peaks through the relevant relationship, desire, risk, or earlier experience. Intense wording cannot replace that preparation. Preserve an effective action or image without immediately explaining its emotional meaning.

Paragraph and section breaks control attention, pauses, speaker changes, and shifts in time or viewpoint. Several identical sentence-length patterns can become monotonous, but variation should follow the scene rather than a fixed numerical schedule.

#### Anti-cliché openings

| Stock opening | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "In a world where..." | Begin with a character's specific action or predicament. |
| "It was a dark and stormy night" | Begin with what a character notices or what the weather changes. |
| "Little did they know..." | Establish dramatic irony through a meaningful difference in knowledge. |
| "Once upon a time" | Use the convention where the tale genuinely requires it; otherwise begin with the relevant event or detail. |

#### Anti-cliché emotion

| Stock wording | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "A chill ran down their spine" | Name a specific reaction or consequential action. |
| "Their heart skipped a beat" | Show what the character does or notices in response. |
| "Tears streamed down their face" | Use tears when they matter; otherwise find the particular expression of grief or resistance to it. |
| "A wave of emotion washed over" | Name the emotion or express it through a precise action. |
| "Their blood ran cold" | Describe the specific fear or its effect. |
| "Butterflies in their stomach" | Use a particular nervous behavior, thought, or perception. |
| "Time stood still" | Describe the narrowed attention or altered experience of duration. |
| "Their world came crashing down" | Establish the realization and its first consequence. |

A particular bodily response can still become repetitive. These replacements are choices, not a requirement to embody every emotion.

#### Anti-cliché description

| Stock wording | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "Piercing blue eyes" | Choose an observed detail that matters to the viewpoint character. |
| "Raven-black hair" | Describe a relevant appearance, movement, or action without a stock beauty formula. |
| "Chiseled features" | Give a distinguishing feature where appearance matters. |
| "A beautiful woman" | Name a specific perception or effect, or retain the direct judgment if that is all the scene needs. |
| "The sun beat down mercilessly" | Describe what the heat changes in the particular environment. |
| "An eerie silence" | Identify the missing sound, remaining sound, or reason for unease. |
| "The room was plunged into darkness" | Clarify what changed and what the character can still perceive. |

Do not invent an asymmetry, smell, or physical defect just to distinguish a character. Specificity should follow the fictional situation.

#### Anti-cliché action

| Stock wording | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "All hell broke loose" | Show the first specific failure and what follows. |
| "They fought for their lives" | Describe the consequential action, mistake, technique, or desperate choice. |
| "With lightning speed" | Give the motion and its effect without a generic speed comparison. |
| "Against all odds" | Establish the actual disadvantage. |
| "In the nick of time" | Show the timing and the consequence of almost being too late. |

#### Anti-cliché dialogue

| Stock wording | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "We need to talk" | Let the speaker begin with the matter they need to raise. |
| "You don't understand" | Make the actual misunderstanding visible. |
| "It's not what it looks like" | Give the speaker's particular explanation, evasion, or lie. |
| "I have a bad feeling about this" | Identify the observation or conflict causing concern. |
| "That went well" used sarcastically | Give the speaker a particular response to what failed. |

A familiar line may be necessary to an established quotation or a specific character interaction. Familiarity alone is not a reason to make everyone speak in conspicuously original lines.

#### Recurrent AI imagery

| Formula | More direct or particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "A tapestry of..." | Name the particular pattern or relationship. |
| "The weight of..." used as an emotional default | Name the burden or choose the particular thought, action, or sensation. |
| "Navigate the complexities" | Establish the difficult choice and its constraints. |
| "In the tapestry of fate" | Remove the generic sentence or state the actual event. |
| "Harbinger of..." | Name the sign and what it predicts. |
| "Cacophony of..." | Identify the relevant sounds. |
| "A symphony of..." | Name the sounds or elements without automatic musical elevation. |
| "The dance of..." | Describe the interaction. |
| "Whispered promises of..." | State what was said, implied, or hoped for. |
| "Echoes of..." | Name the memory, reference, or actual recurring sound. |
| "The fabric of reality" | Describe the particular change to the fictional world's operation. |
| "And so it was that..." | Begin with the action or observation. |

Literal meanings remain available. An actual symphony, whisper, dance, or echo can belong to the scene.

#### Genre conventions without automatic plots

| Genre | Familiar shortcuts requiring a particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Fantasy | An orphan farm child who is automatically the chosen one; a conveniently exact prophecy; evil without motive; uniformly wise elves and gruff dwarves; magic with no cost or limit. |
| Science fiction | Immediate hostility as the default consequence of AI sentience; an unexamined "last man on earth" opening; faster-than-light travel without social consequences; aliens whose differences are merely cosmetic; technology that fails only when convenient. |
| Mystery | Surprise based only on choosing the least suspected person; alcoholism as a detective's ready-made depth; a victim's secret life used as a substitute for development; an accidentally discovered clue that solves the problem; a villain explaining the entire plan. |
| Romance | Hostility changing to love without development; body descriptions substituting for a first encounter; a decisive misunderstanding sustained only by an implausible refusal to speak; an obvious love-triangle choice; a rain kiss used as an automatic emotional climax. |

A convention can work when the story gives it particular causes, consequences, and character meaning. Novelty alone does not repair weak causality, and familiarity does not automatically invalidate a premise.

#### Choose comparisons from the viewpoint

Choose images from the character's world and experience. A merchant might think of a debt collector on payday, while a courtier might think of a fast-spreading rumor. The comparison should reveal the viewpoint as well as the action.

Avoid comparisons that could be attached to any character or situation. A surprising image earns its place through what it reveals, not merely through unfamiliar wording.

A cross-sensory image can convey an unusual perception, as when a sound is described through color or texture. A deliberately strained comparison can express an extreme state of mind. Keep the image intelligible within the scene, and avoid mixing sensory domains merely to sound poetic.

#### Literary techniques

Choose techniques by the effect the passage needs. A device can combine with another, but its classical name does not excuse needless repetition, decorative complexity, or invented logic.

| Purpose | Available techniques | Useful application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Connect sound | Alliteration repeats initial sounds; assonance repeats vowel sounds; consonance repeats consonants. | Establish a restrained sonic relationship among important words. |
| Shape atmosphere through sound | Sibilance emphasizes sibilant sounds; cacophony uses harsh combinations; euphony uses smoother ones. | Support a particular perception without conspicuous sound play. |
| Place a sound in the scene | Onomatopoeia uses words that imitate sounds, such as "buzz" or "crack." | Make a consequential sound perceptible without replacing the event with sound effects. |
| Create a rhyme-like cadence | Similar word endings and internal rhyme can connect neighboring phrases. | Support a ritual, comic utterance, or musical passage without making ordinary narration jingle. |
| Establish a refrain | Anaphora repeats openings; epistrophe repeats endings; symploce combines both. | Develop a meaningful repeated concern or a character's sustained insistence. |
| Connect ideas through repetition | Anadiplosis carries an ending into the next opening; epanalepsis returns to an opening phrase. | Make continuity or a changed implication perceptible. |
| Express insistence | Epizeuxis repeats immediately; diacope separates repeated words with intervening language. | Convey a particular emotional pressure or demand. |
| Characterize through redundancy | Deliberate restatement can reveal evasion, resignation, obsession, or ceremonial speech. | Make the repetition reveal the speaker rather than merely repeat information. |
| Develop a word's meaning | Polyptoton varies grammatical form; ploce and antanaclasis repeat a word with changed senses. | Expose an ambiguity or a character's changing understanding. |
| Articulate contrast | Antithesis balances opposing ideas; paradox and oxymoron express apparent contradiction. | Represent a real tension rather than manufacture an empty opposition. |
| Reverse a relationship | Chiasmus reverses structures or ideas; antimetabole reverses repeated words. | Make a reversal matter to the thought or exchange. |
| Create balance | Isocolon uses parallel units; a tricolon uses three. | Give a justified distinction a deliberate rhythm without forcing its item count. |
| Control emphasis | A periodic sentence delays the main clause; a loose sentence states it first. | Match the sentence's unfolding to expectation, action, or reflection. |
| Control clause movement | Parataxis places independent clauses beside one another; hypotaxis subordinates them. | Express sequence, layered thought, or dependency. |
| Control accumulation | Asyndeton omits conjunctions; polysyndeton adds them. | Create urgency, persistence, or exhaustion where that effect belongs. |
| Build rhetorical intensity | Arrange phrases in increasing importance or force, allowing the sequence to culminate. | Make an important declaration or realization develop instead of beginning every clause at maximum intensity. |
| Represent interruption | Aposiopesis breaks off an utterance; anacoluthon changes its grammatical course. | Reproduce a specific interruption or disrupted thought. |
| Compress recoverable information | Ellipsis omits understood words; apposition identifies a noun through a neighboring phrase. A dispatch may use extreme compression. | Preserve the information its intended reader can recover. A specialized message's brevity does not set the standard for explanatory prose. |
| Sustain an image | Simile makes an explicit comparison; metaphor makes an implicit one; an extended metaphor develops it. | Express a perception particular to the character or situation. |
| Explain through comparison | Analogy relates domains; allegory extends representation across a narrative. | Develop a meaningful relationship while retaining the comparison's limits. |
| Transfer a perception | Personification gives human qualities to something nonhuman; a transferred epithet moves a modifier to an associated object. | Reveal a particular viewpoint without assigning literal intentions. |
| Name by association | Synecdoche uses part and whole; metonymy uses an associated thing; merism invokes a whole through its parts or extremes. | Establish cultural shorthand the reader can follow. |
| Layer knowledge | Dramatic irony gives readers knowledge a character lacks; foreshadowing prepares a later event. | Create fair suspense or a meaningful reinterpretation. |
| Reorder time | A flashback reveals a prior event; a flash-forward changes anticipation. | Make chronology serve disclosure without losing orientation. |
| Enter an event directly | In medias res begins amid consequential action. | Establish involvement, then supply the context readers need. |
| Create irony | Verbal irony separates intended and literal meaning; situational irony reverses established expectations. An opposite-meaning nickname can express a relationship. | Reveal a meaningful discrepancy rather than attach a generic sarcastic comment. |
| Vary emotional scale | Hyperbole exaggerates; litotes and meiosis understate; bathos drops from the important to the trivial. | Express a particular attitude, restraint, or comic reversal. |
| Represent address and desire | Apostrophe addresses an absent person or abstraction; a rhetorical question expresses a stance. A wish or warning reveals what the speaker values or fears. | Give the appeal a credible speaker and specific stakes. |
| Reveal avoidance | Euphemism softens a term; dysphemism makes it harsher; indirect naming can signal taboo or evasion. | Characterize a relationship to the subject without concealing facts in exposition. |
| Draw attention through omission | A speaker can mention something while professing not to discuss it. | Expose evasion or manipulation without making the narration itself habitually indirect. |
| Name through social perception | An epithet, title, or culturally meaningful name can stand for a person or quality. | Reveal how a community or character regards the referent while keeping identity clear. |
| Develop an allusion | A reference to another work, myth, event, or chain of associations can add a second layer of meaning. | Give readers enough context to follow the relevant connection. Obscurity alone adds no depth. |
| Use wordplay | A pun exploits sound or meaning; zeugma or syllepsis gives one governing word different senses. | Create relevant wit rather than make every line conspicuously clever. |
| Build a fictional vocabulary | Neologism coins a word; a portmanteau blends words; a kenning creates a compressed metaphorical name. | Name a distinction the fictional culture actually needs. |
| Shape character diction | Turning a noun into a verb, pairing nouns to express one idea, or inserting a word inside another can distinguish a particular utterance. | Let the usage belong to the speaker and remain understandable. Novel construction is not a substitute for precise meaning. |
| Represent a verbal mistake | A malapropism substitutes a similar-sounding word; a spoonerism transposes sounds. | Characterize a particular moment without stereotyping a group. |
| Focus description | Description can develop an artwork, place, era, season, person's appearance, or pattern of behavior. An extended inventory can express fixation. | Give consequential attention to the object, setting, time, or person. |
| Accumulate detail | Enumeratio lists parts; amplification develops them; distributio assigns different roles or consequences. | Establish relevant scope without turning every passage into an inventory. |
| Reveal thought in motion | Aporia expresses genuine doubt; self-correction changes an initial formulation. | Make uncertainty or revision of judgment intelligible. |
| Reveal a reasoning strategy | An objection may be anticipated, alternatives eliminated, or a contradiction derived from a premise. | Give the reasoning a basis. An unsupported slippery-slope prediction is not a demonstration of contradiction. |
| Constrain a choice | Several available alternatives may each carry a different cost or danger. | Establish the actual constraints. Do not invent a false binary or hide a plausible alternative merely to intensify the dilemma. |
| Expose an unstated assumption | A character's conclusion may rely on a premise their audience takes for granted. | Reveal a worldview or unreliable judgment. A factual argument needs a defensible premise rather than presumed agreement. |
| Represent imagined speech | A character may rehearse a conversation or imagine another person's response. | Reveal anticipation, fear, or judgment while distinguishing imagined words from an actual exchange. |
| Express a worldview concisely | A pointed observation or maxim can reveal a speaker's particular understanding. | Let the insight fit that speaker and moment rather than become an automatic lesson for the reader. |
| Return to an earlier idea | A closing phrase, image, or observation recalls earlier material. | Earn closure by changing its meaning rather than appending a moral. |

Sound, repetition, and syntax may support the same moment. Avoid stacking devices until their construction becomes more noticeable than their effect. Quiet, connective, and deliberately ordinary passages can support the larger experience.

#### Necessary literary exceptions

Metaphor, repetition, fragments, unusual word order, nonstandard grammar, direct address, questions, or exclamations may express a particular character or narrative effect. Their necessity comes from a concrete function such as interrupted speech, rhythm under pressure, cultural language, or an established viewpoint. A proverb, curse, or strategic compliment should likewise belong to a specific speaker and situation.

An interruption in dialogue can require a dash, and an embedded source or formal document can require its own punctuation. Ordinary narrated asides should still follow the general punctuation default. A literary exception is not permission to add em dashes and parenthetical commentary throughout.

Keep dialect coherent and respectful. Do not introduce errors to make narration seem human. Do not add philosophical speeches, bodily metaphors, nonlinear scenes, or rhetorical flourishes merely because the form is storytelling.

#### Worked example

> Before: "A wave of sadness washed over her as she realized that sometimes, the greatest courage is letting go. Her heart ached under the weight of memories."
>
> Illustrative alternative: "She put his spare key in the envelope. For a while, she kept her hand on it."

The alternative creates a specific fictional action and leaves its emotional implication available. It is one possible scene, not a translation of every kind of sadness. A direct emotion label may suit another scene better.
