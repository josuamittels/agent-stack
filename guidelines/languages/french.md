# French Prose Writing – Best Practices

Natural French makes the intended meaning available without unnecessary effort. Its vocabulary, grammar, sentence movement, and organization fit the subject, reader, situation, and form. It can be formal, conversational, concise, analytical, or literary. Native phrasing does not require elaborate abstraction, metropolitan slang, ceremonial introductions, or a uniformly elegant surface.

Preserve meaning, facts, necessary terminology, and the integrity of quotations. Apply the general conventions throughout. The style sections add requirements and narrowly necessary exceptions for academic, technical, absolute, article, and storytelling prose.

Express relationships in French rather than reproducing English words and sentence shapes. French has its own means of expressing reference, emphasis, social distance, uncertainty, and temporal perspective. Grammatical correctness, idiomatic expression, and appropriateness to an occasion are related but distinct qualities.

Presentation follows French conventions with a restrained house style. Straight quotation marks and the exclusion of sentence dashes are house preferences. No individual word, construction, punctuation mark, or degree of complexity establishes authorship.

Examples illustrate writing choices. They do not report actual findings, events, product behavior, or measurements. An alternative that invents an action demonstrates a possible fictional scene rather than an interchangeable paraphrase.

## Meaning, reader, and register

### Preserve the proposition

Keep what a sentence says, whom or what it concerns, and how strongly it says it. A smoother revision must preserve conditions, exclusions, quantities, causality, chronology, attribution, and uncertainty. Shortening helps only when the relevant meaning survives.

| Original | Meaning-changing revision | Faithful revision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Le traitement peut réduire les symptômes chez certains patients." | "Le traitement réduit les symptômes." | "Chez certains patients, le traitement peut réduire les symptômes." |
| "Seuls les administrateurs sont autorisés à supprimer les dossiers archivés." | "Les administrateurs sont autorisés à supprimer les dossiers." | "La suppression des dossiers archivés est réservée aux administrateurs." |
| "L'équipe a signalé moins d'erreurs après la modification." | "La modification a évité les erreurs." | "Après la modification, l'équipe a signalé moins d'erreurs." |
| "Il était fatigué et ne voulait pas parler." | "Épuisé, il préférait le silence." | "Il était fatigué. Il ne voulait pas parler." |
| "Cette explication pourrait s'appliquer aux chiens qui vivent avec d'autres chiens." | "Ce comportement est fréquent chez les animaux qui vivent avec d'autres animaux domestiques." | "Pour les chiens qui vivent avec d'autres chiens, cette explication pourrait convenir." |

Keep permission, technical capability, and possibility distinct. A smoother sentence must not turn authorization into ability or a possible outcome into an entitlement.

Do not strengthen fatigue into exhaustion, a possible account into observed prevalence, or sequence into causation. A vivid verb can change the facts as easily as an omitted qualification.

Preserve deliberate ambiguity where it serves a literary work. Remove accidental ambiguity from explanations, instructions, and factual claims. An uncertain motive differs from an instruction with an unidentified object.

### Write for the reader's actual knowledge

Establish unfamiliar concepts before relying on them. Explain a necessary term near its first relevant use. Omit elementary background the audience can reasonably be expected to know.

> Opaque for a general reader: "L'idempotence garantit la robustesse des nouvelles tentatives."
>
> Clearer: "Renvoyer la même requête ne crée pas de deuxième commande."

Retain the term when useful: "La requête est idempotente : la renvoyer ne crée pas de deuxième commande." The explanation depends on that behavior being established. It does not promise that every retry succeeds.

Use concrete examples to introduce difficult ideas, then connect them to the general point where needed. Specialist vocabulary alone and excessive elementary explanation can both obstruct comprehension.

Give unfamiliar abbreviations their full form at first use, followed by the abbreviation when it will recur. In a short passage, the full term alone can be clearer. Familiarity depends on the readers, not on the writer's profession.

An abstract, caption, table note, or isolated interface message needs sufficient context to stand alone. Define an unfamiliar abbreviation separately when readers may encounter either element without the other.

### Match register to the form

Use precise formal language for scholarly claims, literal language for procedures, and character-appropriate diction for fiction. Natural French does not require rhetorical questions, first-person anecdotes, slang, or a friendly opening.

Written standard French can be approachable without transcribing speech. Omission of negative "ne", forms such as "t'as", and colloquial vocabulary belong to particular voices and settings. They are not general signs of authenticity.

Ordinary elisions and contractions such as "l'application", "j'ai", "au", and "du" are grammatical forms, not informal shortcuts to expand in formal prose.

Formality comes from precise relationships and proportionate claims. Repeated "il convient de", "dans le cadre de", and "la présente" do not make a claim more rigorous. A conventional legal or technical expression can nevertheless carry a distinction that a casual paraphrase loses.

Maintain a coherent register rather than moving between inflated administration and forced familiarity. An informal greeting cannot compensate for an unclear demand.

### Keep address and social distance coherent

Choose "tu" or "vous" according to the established relationship, audience, and medium. "Vous" can address one person formally or several people. "Tu" addresses one person informally.

Keep verbs, possessives, and commands coherent with that relationship. Do not alternate address forms to create variety.

| Relationship | Coherent wording |
| --- | --- |
| Informal singular | "Tu peux compléter ta demande plus tard." |
| Formal singular | "Vous pouvez compléter votre demande plus tard." |
| Plural | "Vous pouvez compléter vos demandes plus tard." |
| Neutral action label | "Compléter la demande" |

The plural example concerns several applications. Use singular or plural objects according to the actual ownership and task, not merely the grammatical form of address.

With polite singular "vous", the verb uses its plural form while an adjective or participle referring to the person normally remains singular: "Vous êtes prêt"; "Vous êtes prête". With several people, number and the applicable agreement change. Do not infer a person's identity to fill an unnecessary gendered expression.

Formal address can be warm, and informal address can be patronizing. Respect depends on the wording, burden, and relationship. An ordinary instruction may need only a direct action; a discretionary request may need a reason.

Avoid multiplying "veuillez", "merci de", and "s'il vous plaît" to create politeness mechanically. "Merci de" can function as a directive rather than an expression of gratitude; use it with an appropriate intention.

Pronouns generally remain lowercase unless sentence position or a particular naming or publication convention requires otherwise. Do not import German courtesy capitalization.

### Use the intended variety of French

Use a coherent variety appropriate to the readers. France, Quebec, Belgium, Switzerland, and other francophone contexts have legitimate lexical, institutional, stylistic, and typographic differences. Do not present metropolitan usage as the only native form or mix regional markers randomly.

Choose a regionally established term when it names the right thing for those readers. "Courriel" and "e-mail", or "soixante-dix" and "septante", can reflect different contexts. Neither a technical term nor a number form should become a pretext for an invented local personality.

For an unspecified broad audience, prefer widely understood standard wording. Explain unfamiliar institutions rather than replacing them with supposedly equivalent organizations from another country.

Distinguish language localization from factual substitution. A Quebec institution, Swiss qualification, or Belgian location retains its actual identity even when the prose addresses another audience.

Do not infer politeness, humor, knowledge, or personal preference from nationality alone. Regional dialogue and dialect need a coherent speaker and setting.

Preserve an established orthographic convention in supplied prose. Traditional and rectified spellings include accepted alternatives; do not label a valid form an error merely because another convention differs. Apply the chosen convention coherently without altering proper names or faithful quotations.

### Describe people accurately and respectfully

Use names, pronouns, and descriptions relevant to the subject. Respect stated identity preferences. Do not infer identity from appearance, a name, or membership in a group.

Mention age, disability, ethnicity, nationality, sex, gender, or another personal characteristic when it contributes necessary information. Avoid stereotypes, demeaning labels, and wording that reduces a person to a diagnosis.

Person-first and identity-first formulations can both be appropriate. Follow known individual or community preferences without assuming unanimity. An accepted name for a group should not erase the distinctions the subject requires.

Distinguish grammatical gender from a person's identity. A grammatically feminine noun such as "personne" does not establish the person's gender. Possessive forms agree with the possessed noun, not with an assumed male or female possessor.

Use a known individual's stated pronouns and relevant forms. French neutral pronouns and agreement practices vary; do not mechanically translate singular English pronouns or impose one innovation on every reader.

Neutral role descriptions, collective nouns, plural formulations, and direct address can help where they preserve the intended group and responsibility. Paired forms, professional-title feminization, and abbreviated inclusive forms have different institutional and regional conventions. Follow the applicable context without declaring one system universally required or universally invalid.

Do not let a neutral rewrite change who is included. "Les personnes inscrites" does not necessarily mean all residents, applicants, or employees.

In research, distinguish sex from gender where it affects meaning. Name what was actually recorded, preserve self-descriptions, and retain the data's limits.

> Vague: "Les personnes âgées ne comprenaient pas le service."
>
> More specific, if supported: "Les participants de 70 à 84 ans ont signalé qu'ils ne pouvaient pas lire les petits caractères."

The alternative requires those ages and reports to be established. Respectful language does not justify invented precision or stronger conclusions.

### Preserve voice without preserving avoidable confusion

A voice may depend on restraint, expansiveness, dry humor, directness, or characteristic movement of thought. Preserve meaningful features while removing accidental repetition, faulty grammar, unsupported claims, and obscure phrasing.

Multilingual writers can retain distinctive emphasis, perspective, and experience in idiomatic French. Correct unclear constructions without adding fashionable colloquialisms, invented cultural references, or a generic francophone persona.

Do not introduce mistakes, rare vocabulary, arbitrary fragments, random digressions, or artificial irregularity to imitate a person. Polished prose can be natural; unpolished prose can be formulaic. Deliberate speech patterns belong to their particular voice rather than to every surrounding sentence.

## Evidence, specificity, and uncertainty

### State the concrete claim

Say what happened, what something does, what changes, or what the reader needs to know. Replace atmospheric reassurance with an established mechanism, consequence, instruction, or number.

> Vague: "Le nouveau parcours offre une expérience utilisateur fluide et sans friction."
>
> Concrete, if established: "Si l'envoi échoue, les données saisies restent dans le formulaire."

Specificity comes from the material. A plausible mechanism or product behavior is not a fact. If implementation is unknown, describe the established behavior without inventing how it works.

### Separate observation, attribution, and interpretation

Distinguish observed events, attributed accounts, and conclusions about either. Identify whose judgment a statement expresses.

| Kind of statement | Illustrative wording |
| --- | --- |
| Observation | "Trois participants se sont arrêtés avant la dernière question." |
| Attributed account | "Les participants ont jugé la dernière question peu claire." |
| Interpretation | "La formulation de la question pourrait avoir contribué aux abandons." |

The interpretation needs a defensible connection to the evidence. Do not turn an explanation offered by one person into the established cause or an impression into consensus.

A possible function does not establish frequency. Keep the population and kind of claim intact. An explanation compatible with dogs living together is not a general observation about all animals.

### Name the source of a claim

Replace borrowed authority such as "les experts s'accordent à dire", "des études prouvent", and "certains observateurs estiment" with an identified source and relevant claim where available. Otherwise remove the pretended authority or state the uncertainty.

Attach a reference to the proposition it supports. A real source can be misused through inaccurate paraphrase, a wider population, an omitted condition, or a stronger causal conclusion. Separate its findings from the writer's inference.

Distinguish preprints, published studies, datasets, and secondary accounts. Publication status describes the source; it does not establish truth on its own. Reputation and citation counts do not replace relevant evidence.

Keep titles, authors, dates, identifiers, findings, quotations, and locators consistent with the version cited. Do not attach a preprint's result to a later article's details when the versions differ.

Names should locate an influence, claim, or evidence rather than provide unexplained prestige. An attributed claim needs a recognizable connection to the actual material.

### Attribute reported speech without changing its status

Use reporting verbs according to the relation actually expressed. "A déclaré", "a indiqué", "a reconnu", "a nié", and "a prétendu" are not neutral synonyms. A skeptical or conceding verb needs that function.

Indirect speech requires appropriate changes of person, temporal reference, and sometimes tense.

> Direct statement: "Les données sont complètes."
>
> Report: "La chercheuse affirme que les données sont complètes."
>
> Past reporting frame: "La chercheuse a affirmé que les données étaient complètes."

The past reporting frame does not by itself imply that the data are now incomplete. Retain a present tense when continuing validity is deliberately asserted and the context supports it. Do not backshift mechanically or erase the distinction between past knowledge and present assertion.

Maintain the attribution boundary across a longer passage. Readers should know whether a sentence continues a person's account or states what the reporting voice establishes.

A conditional can mark reported or unconfirmed information: "Le bâtiment aurait été évacué". It does not identify the source on its own. Name the source and the actual uncertainty where relevant rather than replacing attribution with repeated conditionals.

### Make numerical comparisons interpretable

Identify the population, timeframe, units, denominator, and baseline. Name what was counted or measured. A percentage of respondents differs from a percentage of all eligible people; counts differ from rates.

| Distinction | Writing requirement | Illustrative wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Count and rate | Supply relevant population sizes or comparable rates. | "La zone A a enregistré 40 incidents pour 10 000 habitants, contre 60 pour 30 000 dans la zone B." The rates are 4 and 2 per 1,000 residents. |
| Percentage and percentage points | Separate relative change from the difference between percentages. | "La proportion est passée de 20 % à 30 %." That is 10 percentage points and a 50% relative increase. |
| Mean and median | Name the summary actually used. | "Le délai médian était de douze minutes" does not report the arithmetic mean. |
| Money over time | Distinguish nominal amounts from inflation-adjusted values. | "Les dépenses ont augmenté en valeur nominale, mais ont baissé en euros constants de 2024." Both comparisons require evidence. |

Use comparable periods, boundaries, definitions, and populations. Explain a material change in coverage or classification rather than presenting it as a change in the underlying phenomenon. Pair a striking relative increase with its starting value or absolute size where scale matters.

The arithmetic mean is the total divided by the number of observations. The median is the middle ordered value, or the midpoint of the two central values for an even number of observations. Extreme values can shift the mean without a comparable median change. Avoid an ambiguous average where the choice affects interpretation.

Do not give more precision than the measurement supports. Preserve values when adapting presentation; number and unit formatting belongs under punctuation and presentation.

### Distinguish magnitude, precision, and importance

An effect's size, estimate precision, statistical significance, and practical importance answer different questions. Explain consequences in relevant units. A small effect can be estimated precisely; an important possible effect can remain uncertain.

Identify an interval's type and level where relevant. Interpret it according to the method. A confidence interval is not a range containing a stated proportion of individual observations.

A nonsignificant result does not establish absence or equivalence. State what the evidence can distinguish.

> Overstated for an inconclusive comparison: "Les groupes ne différaient pas."
>
> Bounded, if imprecision is the issue: "L'estimation était trop imprécise pour établir si les groupes différaient dans une mesure pertinente en pratique."

Use the alternative only where that explanation fits the analysis. It is not a replacement for every nonsignificant finding. Equivalence needs evidence suited to that claim.

### Keep the level of inference clear

A relationship among schools, regions, or institutions does not establish the same relationship among individuals. State the level at which evidence was collected and the conclusion applies.

Aggregate findings can conceal subgroup differences or change when group proportions change. Explain material discrepancies rather than choosing the level favoring the conclusion. Do not invent subgroup patterns.

Statistical adjustment alone does not establish causality. Sequence, prediction, and a plausible mechanism also need suitable evidence. Relevant alternatives may include selection, confounding, reverse causation, and regression toward the mean.

Describe actual inferential limits without burying every statement in indiscriminate caveats. Retain alternatives that materially affect the account.

### Explain the step from evidence to conclusion

Explain why the evidence supports the claim. A quotation, statistic, or citation may still need an account of its relevance. "Par conséquent" cannot supply a missing premise.

Represent opposing arguments accurately. Include material counterevidence and plausible alternatives, explaining relevant differences in measurement, population, method, or setting. Avoid selective reporting, personal attacks, and artificial balance between unequally supported positions.

Distinguish findings, supported explanations, and speculation. Compatibility with an explanation does not uniquely establish it. Name a consequential assumption instead of concealing it in a confident transition.

### Anchor time-sensitive claims

Use a date, period, or version where "actuellement", "récemment", and "la dernière version" would become ambiguous. Preserve an earlier institutional role or its dates where that relationship matters.

Distinguish the date of an event from a later account's publication. Do not infer chronology from a source's presentation order or imply influence from material that did not yet exist.

> Time-dependent: "La dernière version prévoit une exception."
>
> Anchored, if established: "La version de 2024 ajoute une exception à la règle de 2020."

A date improves specificity only when accurate. Keep proposals, adopted decisions, and effective dates distinct.

### Prefer evidence to evaluative adjectives

"Important", "impressionnant", "puissant", "efficace", and "significatif" need a basis. State the result or consequence where available. "Statistiquement significatif" names a defined statistical finding without establishing practical importance.

> Unsupported: "La modification améliore considérablement les performances."
>
> Illustrative measurement: "Dans le test de référence, le temps médian de traitement est passé de 120 à 90 millisecondes."

Identify comparison conditions affecting the result. Where no measurement exists, describe established behavior without implying a measured improvement.

### Preserve useful qualifications and modal meanings

Remove stacked uncertainty without removing uncertainty itself. One "peut" may replace overlapping hedges; it cannot replace a condition determining whether the claim holds.

> Padded: "Il pourrait éventuellement être possible que l'effet soit seulement temporaire."
>
> Direct: "L'effet peut être temporaire."

Keep relevant bounds such as "dans cet échantillon" and "à basse température". Do not replace a possibility with a prediction or a recommendation with a requirement to sound decisive.

Conditional forms can express hypotheses, politeness, future-in-the-past meanings, or unconfirmed reporting. "Je voudrais un renseignement" and "Il a dit qu'il viendrait" do not express the same kind of doubt.

Modal verbs also have distinct practical and epistemic functions. "Il doit travailler" differs from "Il doit être arrivé". "Vous n'êtes pas obligé de venir" removes an obligation; "Vous ne devez pas venir" normally expresses a prohibition in an instruction. Make the distinction explicit when consequences matter.

Do not delete meaningful "souvent", "en principe", or "probablement" because a direct style is desired. Explain material exceptions instead of using vague qualification to hide them.

### Make examples carry information

An example should demonstrate a principle, expose a limit, or distinguish similar cases. Explain its connection where necessary without stating the implication twice.

A single case may illustrate an experience without establishing prevalence. Distinguish anecdotes, counterexamples, and evidence for a general conclusion.

Identify hypothetical scenarios in factual prose. In fiction, invented details belong to the established world. Plausibility does not authorize invented reporting, quotations, measurements, or mechanisms.

### Give quotations a purpose

Quote when wording supplies evidence, reveals perspective, or matters to the argument. Identify the speaker and context. Do not leave a quotation to replace reasoning.

Paraphrase when exact wording adds little. Preserve scope, certainty, and attribution. Keeping a source's structure while substituting words can still reproduce its expression too closely.

Attribute borrowed ideas as well as words. Identify indirect citations rather than implying direct access to unconsulted material. Retain required page, section, or paragraph locators.

Distinguish translated quotations from original French wording where it matters. A fluent translation may adjust syntax without adding explanations, correcting the speaker's reasoning, or changing the claim.

## Organization and paragraph development

### Begin with something useful

Open with the claim, situation, observation, event, or genuine question that gives the piece a purpose. Remove greetings, declarations about the modern world, and announcements that the text will explore its subject.

> Padded: "Dans un monde en constante évolution, les sauvegardes sont plus importantes que jamais."
>
> Direct: "Une sauvegarde n'est utile que si les données peuvent être restaurées."

An opening can be quiet. It needs relevance rather than manufactured drama. A question should express an uncertainty the piece addresses, not imply an unsupported allegation.

### Let the subject determine the structure

Arrange material around purpose and dependencies. Procedures follow action order; arguments develop reasons; references support lookup; narratives arrange experience and disclosure.

Do not impose a prefabricated introduction, three equal sections, and recap. Use the number and size of sections the subject requires.

A thesis, antithesis, and synthesis can serve a particular academic exercise. They are not compulsory forms for every French explanation, essay, report, or article. An exploratory argument should not manufacture opposing camps to fill an outline.

Do not assume native French requires a longer introduction than the material warrants. Supply background where it enables understanding rather than delaying the useful point.

### Give each paragraph a distinct contribution

A paragraph can establish a point, develop it, supply evidence, qualify it, connect ideas, or create a narrative effect. Keep sentences together when they develop one movement.

Break paragraphs for meaningful changes of subject, speaker, time, argumentative function, or attention. Do not isolate every sentence for emphasis or preserve a dense block merely because all sentences share a broad theme.

Details may establish context, reveal a limitation, or complicate the argument without restating the central message. Repeated explanation can resemble depth while adding nothing. Supply a missing reason, example, condition, consequence, or alternative where needed; otherwise stop.

### Connect ideas through their relationship

Make each sentence's contribution recognizable: consequence, contrast, concession, explanation, sequence, or a new subject. Use a connector when it expresses that relationship.

| Relationship | Suitable expressions where the relationship holds |
| --- | --- |
| Reason or cause | "parce que", "car", "puisque" |
| Consequence | "donc", "par conséquent", "de sorte que" |
| Concession | "bien que", "pourtant", "néanmoins" |
| Contrast | "mais", "en revanche", "tandis que" |
| Sequence | "ensuite", "puis", "avant de" |

These expressions differ in syntax, presupposition, and scope. A known reason introduced by "puisque" is not always equivalent to a new explanation with "parce que". A purpose differs from a realized consequence even when related wording can introduce both.

> Mechanical: "De plus, le service conserve des journaux. En outre, il enregistre les erreurs."
>
> Connected, if both describe the same behavior: "Le service enregistre les erreurs dans ses journaux."

Use sequence markers for real sequences. Repeated "et" can hide dependencies; repeated "ensuite" can give every event equal weight.

Cohesion also comes from maintaining topics, stable terminology, and development of established concepts. Not every paragraph needs "en effet", "cependant", or "ainsi". More elevated transitions do not repair a missing connection.

### Develop enough, then stop

Concision retains what readers need in less unnecessary language. It does not reduce a substantial thought to a generic label.

"Le livre était intéressant" may need an explanation of what interested the writer. A paragraph that already demonstrates a point may need no summary. Match development to the reader's task and the cost of misunderstanding.

Do not give every short section a miniature introduction and conclusion. A heading can establish its subject; the next sentence should contribute information.

### Preserve complexity that belongs to the subject

Represent real exceptions, competing motives, uncertainty, and costs. Avoid forcing a tidy binary or universal lesson onto material that does not support one.

Reduce linguistic difficulty while preserving conceptual complexity. Clear prose can explain why a problem remains unresolved. It need not invent a solution.

### Avoid manufactured contrasts and false ranges

State the point without rejecting an alternative nobody proposed. "Il ne s'agit pas de X, mais de Y" and "non seulement X, mais aussi Y" serve genuine distinctions; repeated automatically, they create theatrical emphasis.

> Staged: "Il ne s'agit pas seulement de données. Il s'agit de personnes."
>
> Concrete where supported: "Des adresses incorrectes peuvent empêcher les destinataires de recevoir les décisions qui les concernent."

The alternative makes a particular claim requiring evidence. It is not a universal paraphrase of the staged contrast.

Use "de X à Y" for a meaningful range, scale, or progression. "De la collaboration à l'innovation" does not establish one if the expressions are simply two topics. Name the actual subjects and relationship.

### Let substance determine the wording

A piece's strength depends on what it includes, omits, relates, and concludes. Replacing suspect words cannot repair missing evidence, an empty argument, or an unearned narrative turn.

Do not rename one assertion repeatedly to make it resemble several insights. Avoid invented objections and false choices used only to give a paragraph movement.

### End where the material has earned an ending

An ending can answer the opening question, establish a consequence, name uncertainty, or identify a justified action. Remove sentences that merely repeat the introduction or praise the future.

Avoid automatic "En conclusion, il convient de souligner", uplifting forecasts, engagement questions, and generic calls to action. A synthesis belongs where it connects findings readers need to hold together, not merely where a text appears to need a formal finish.

A narrative may close with an action or image without an explanatory moral. Factual prose can end with a limited conclusion rather than an invented remedy.

## Sentence structure and syntax

### Give the sentence an identifiable subject and action

Keep the relevant actor, action, and object easy to locate. Ordinary French declarative clauses generally put the subject before the conjugated verb, but other constructions can serve emphasis, presentation, or a particular register.

> Dense: "La réalisation de l'ajustement de la limite de stockage intervient, après examen des fichiers actuellement présents dans le projet, par l'intermédiaire du fichier de configuration."
>
> Clearer: "Les fichiers présents dans le projet sont d'abord examinés. La limite de stockage est ensuite ajustée dans le fichier de configuration."

The revision retains the preliminary examination instead of dropping it. If a person or program performs the adjustment, name that actor only when established. Do not invent agency to avoid the passive.

Keep a long opening from concealing the predicate. A sentence about responsibility should identify who is responsible; a sentence about a process can legitimately foreground it.

### Use French clause order rather than source-language order

An ordinary initial time phrase does not trigger Germanic verb-second inversion.

> "Demain, l'équipe examinera la demande."
>
> "Après la réunion, l'équipe examinera la demande."

Do not put the verb before the subject merely because an opening constituent precedes it. French has its own inversion constructions, including questions and particular literary or presentational patterns.

Subordinate clauses do not acquire Germanic verb-final order. Keep a normal French clause after the relevant connector: "parce que l'équipe examine la demande". English word order is also not a sufficient guide to object-pronoun placement, adjective position, or the distribution of complements.

Preserve the grammatical subject where required. A subject omitted in another language may need an explicit French pronoun or noun. Imperatives and recoverable coordination have their own patterns.

### Choose questions according to register and function

Questions can use inversion, "est-ce que", or declarative order with interrogative intonation represented by punctuation. These constructions have different register and contextual effects.

> "Avez-vous reçu le dossier ?"
>
> "Est-ce que vous avez reçu le dossier ?"
>
> "Vous avez reçu le dossier ?"

The last is especially natural in conversational contexts; it need not become the default for a formal questionnaire. Inversion can be appropriate without making every short interface prompt conspicuously formal.

Indirect questions normally use subordinate-clause order and do not take a question mark merely because their content is a question: "Nous ignorons quand le dossier arrivera." Do not insert "est-ce que" mechanically inside every indirect question.

Ask a question where the reader actually needs to answer or consider one. Do not use a sequence of questions to create artificial engagement when a clear statement would serve.

### Use emphasis and presentation with a purpose

French can foreground information through constructions such as "c'est ... qui", "c'est ... que", and a topic followed by a resumptive pronoun.

> "C'est Léa qui a envoyé le dossier."
>
> "Ce dossier, je l'ai déjà lu."

The first singles out an actor; the second establishes a topic with a characteristic emphatic or conversational movement. Neither is a neutral replacement for every subject-first sentence.

Use emphasis to mark a real distinction. Repeated "c'est précisément", "ce qui compte", and topic resumptions can make ordinary points seem staged.

Choose presentational forms according to the actual referent. "Il y a", "voici", "c'est", and "il est" are not interchangeable translations of an English opening pronoun. "C'est une ingénieure" and "Elle est ingénieure" use different structures for identification.

### Express one main thought without imposing one sentence length

A sentence can carry a claim with its condition, explanation, or consequence. Split it when several competing claims or nested dependencies force the reader to reconstruct the structure.

> Overloaded: "Le serveur a rejeté la requête, dans laquelle le jeton manquait, et le client a réessayé alors que le compte était entre-temps bloqué, ce qui a provoqué un nouvel échec."
>
> Clearer, if both causes are established: "Le serveur a rejeté la requête parce que le jeton manquait. Le client a réessayé. Cette tentative a échoué parce que le compte était alors bloqué."

Do not select an uncertain causal interpretation merely to improve syntax. If a reason is unknown, retain that uncertainty.

Long sentences can sustain a connected explanation. Replacing all subordination with short main clauses can make reasoning choppy and less explicit. French does not require either perpetual brevity or elaborate periods.

### Use active, passive, and impersonal forms according to perspective

Use active voice when responsibility matters: "L'équipe examine la demande." Use passive voice when the process, affected object, or unknown actor is the relevant focus: "La demande est examinée."

Do not invent an actor to remove a passive. Avoid hiding a known actor when readers need to know who acts.

An impersonal construction can name an actual event or requirement: "Il reste deux places"; "Il faut une pièce d'identité". Repeated "il est procédé à" and "il convient de" may obscure a simple action, but every introductory "il" is not filler.

"On" can refer to an indefinite actor, people generally, or an established group. It is not inherently casual or a synonym for every passive. "On a supprimé le dossier" can conceal responsibility if the actor matters.

Use "nous" for an identified group rather than an invented institutional voice. Reflexive or middle constructions such as "Ce tissu se lave à froid" have their own meanings; they do not automatically describe completed actions.

Distinguish process, completed event, and resulting state through tense and context. Do not mechanically label every "être" plus participle as a passive that needs rewriting.

### Make clause relationships explicit

Coordinate ideas of comparable weight. Subordinate a reason, condition, concession, or dependency where that relationship is established.

> Sequence only: "La pile était vide et l'alarme n'a pas sonné."
>
> Cause, if known: "L'alarme n'a pas sonné parce que la pile était vide."

Do not invent causality to make a sentence tighter. Distinguish an intended purpose from an achieved consequence. Related constructions may require different moods according to that meaning.

"Si" can introduce a condition or an indirect yes-or-no question: "Vérifiez si le fichier existe" asks for a determination. "Vérifiez le fichier s'il existe" makes the action conditional.

A trailing "ce qui", "ainsi", or "permettant de" needs an identifiable relationship. Name the proposition where an appended clause could refer to several earlier claims.

### Combine related actions without adding detail

Combine short sentences when they describe one movement and separate emphasis is unnecessary.

> Repetitive: "Elle est allée au marché. Elle a acheté des légumes. Elle est rentrée."
>
> Connected: "Elle est allée au marché, a acheté des légumes et est rentrée."

Do not add freshness, speed, mood, or a motive merely to improve flow. Retain an actor where a shared subject could become ambiguous.

Coordination must preserve required prepositions and compatible complements. Two verbs cannot always share an object or pronoun just because their English counterparts can. Repeat the necessary element where omission changes its grammatical role.

### State the claim hidden in an appended phrase

Expressions such as "garantissant", "favorisant", "mettant en évidence", and "témoignant de" can append an unsupported benefit or interpretation.

> Empty addition: "Le service conserve le fichier, garantissant ainsi une expérience sans friction."
>
> Direct: "Le service conserve le fichier."

Where an appended phrase contains a substantive consequence, state and support that relationship. Replacing the adjective without repairing the inference leaves the problem intact.

Useful participial constructions remain available when their actor and relation are clear. Do not reproduce the English trailing-participle pattern throughout an explanation merely because it can be translated.

### Keep modifiers and their scope clear

Place limiting words so the intended restriction is intelligible. Position, emphasis, and context can all matter.

> "Seule Léa vérifie le dossier."
>
> "Léa ne vérifie que le dossier."

The first limits the actor; the second restricts what she checks. If the claim instead means that checking is her only action, state that explicitly.

Preserve distinctions involving "déjà", "encore", "presque", "à peine", "seulement", and time expressions. Moving one for rhythm can alter chronology, degree, or expectation.

Do not place an adverb where it appears to modify the wrong verb or proposition. Name the intended relation rather than expecting a distant qualifier to limit an entire complicated sentence.

### Give reduced clauses a recoverable actor

Infinitive, participial, and gerund constructions can compress information where the actor and relationship are clear.

> Misattached: "En arrivant à la gare, la pluie s'est mise à tomber."
>
> Clear: "Quand je suis arrivé à la gare, la pluie s'est mise à tomber."

The first accidentally links arrival to the rain. Use a full clause where readers would assign the action incorrectly. Do not translate every English initial participle with "en" plus a participle.

"Pour" plus an infinitive and "pour que" plus a clause differ in how their subject is supplied. "Je pars tôt pour arriver à l'heure" normally keeps the same actor; a different actor may require an explicit clause.

Distinguish manner, time, reason, condition, and consequence. A compact construction must not leave readers guessing which relationship is intended.

### Give personal and possessive pronouns clear referents

"Il", "elle", "ils", "elles", "ce", "cela", and "ça" need recognizable reference. Agreement and proximity do not always resolve competing antecedents.

> Ambiguous: "Quand le client a contacté le serveur, il a redémarré."
>
> Clear, if that is the event: "Le serveur a redémarré quand le client l'a contacté."

Repeat a noun when it identifies the intended actor more clearly than a pronoun. Do not replace every repeated technical noun merely for elegance.

"Cela" or "ce" can refer to a proposition; naming it may be clearer when several previous statements are candidates. "Ça" fits many conversational contexts but is not automatically equivalent in register to every "cela".

Possessives follow the possessed noun. "Son dossier" does not identify a male owner, and "sa demande" does not identify a female owner. Where ownership is unclear, name the person or role.

Choose stressed forms such as "moi", "toi", "lui", "elle", "nous", "vous", and "eux" where their construction requires them. Do not use an English object position as the guide to a French pronoun form.

### Place object pronouns according to the construction

Clitic pronouns normally stand before the verb they depend on, with their established order.

> "Je le lui envoie."
>
> "Je ne le lui ai pas envoyé."
>
> "Je vais le lui envoyer."

In the third example, the pronouns depend on the infinitive, not simply on the first verb in the sentence. Other constructions have their own dependencies; do not move all pronouns together mechanically.

An affirmative imperative places the relevant pronouns after the verb and uses grammatical hyphens: "Envoyez-le-moi." A negative imperative restores preverbal placement: "Ne me l'envoyez pas."

Distinguish subject and object functions and direct from indirect complements. "Je le vois" and "Je lui parle" follow the respective verbs. "Lui" as an indirect clitic can refer to a woman as well as a man.

Avoid imitating English postverbal object-pronoun order or a spoken nonstandard cluster in ordinary edited prose. A difficult cluster can be recast naturally with a named noun while preserving the relationship.

### Use "en" and "y" for a clear grammatical function

"En" and "y" are ordinary resources for reference and complementation, not removable words without English equivalents.

| Construction | Illustrative wording | Relevant contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantity with "en" | "J'en ai trois." | Refers to an established countable category while retaining the quantity. |
| Complement with "en" | "Nous en avons parlé." | Refers to an established topic introduced by a suitable "de" construction. |
| Place with "y" | "Elle y travaille." | Refers to a known place. |
| Complement with "y" | "J'y pense." | Refers to an established object of the appropriate construction. |

Do not omit a required pronoun because the translated sentence seems shorter. Do not insert one merely because the source contains a location word.

Reference to people can require a personal form such as "à elle" or "de lui"; actual usage depends on the verb and context. "Y" and "en" are not universal substitutes for any preposition plus noun.

Keep the antecedent recognizable. Where a short pronoun could refer to several topics, name the intended one. A referentially economical sentence is useful only if its reference can be recovered.

### Use articles and partitives naturally

French normally uses determiners according to its own constructions, not English article omission. Generic reference can require an article: "La patience est utile." Profession after "être" can use another pattern: "Elle est médecin."

Distinguish definite, indefinite, and partitive reference: "le pain", "un pain", and "du pain" do not always name the same quantity or object.

In many negative direct-object constructions, an indefinite or partitive reduces to "de": "Je n'ai pas de dossier"; "Elle ne boit pas de café". Do not extend this mechanically to definite reference, contrast, attributes after "être", or restriction: "Ce n'est pas un problème"; "Je ne bois que du café".

### Use contractions and elision naturally

Use grammatical contractions such as "au", "aux", "du", and "des" where required. Do not mistake object pronouns for articles when contracting a preposition.

Elide where the construction requires it: "l'application", "j'arrive", "qu'il". An aspirated "h" can block elision: "le haricot". Initial spelling alone does not decide every case.

Use appropriate forms before vowels where required: "mon adresse", "cet outil", "un bel exemple". These are grammatical adjustments, not changes in the referent's identity.

### Preserve gender and number agreement

Maintain agreement among determiner, noun, adjective, pronoun, and verb where required. A change of noun, number, or sentence structure can require several corresponding changes.

> "une demande complète"
>
> "des demandes complètes"
>
> "Le dossier est complet."

Grammatical gender does not authorize assumptions about personal identity. Preserve exact names and necessary labels rather than inflecting an identifier.

Keep subject-verb agreement clear when material intervenes. A nearby plural noun does not automatically determine the verb. A collective expression may require attention to whether the grammar and meaning concern the group or its members.

Honorific address and particular pronouns can involve semantic agreement, as explained under register. Do not let a mechanical number rule override the actual referent.

With "on", the finite verb remains singular. When it refers to an identified group, an adjective or participle can reflect the group's actual number and applicable agreement: "On est arrivées" can refer to a group of women. Do not apply that plural automatically to indefinite "on" or invent the group's identity to choose an ending.

### Keep participle and pronominal-verb agreement accurate

With ordinary "être" constructions, past participles commonly agree with their subject: "Elle est partie"; "Ils sont arrivés". With "avoir", a preceding direct object can determine agreement: "Les lettres que j'ai envoyées".

Do not treat every noun before "avoir" as a direct object or every pronominal verb as an automatic subject-agreement case.

> "Elles se sont rencontrées."
>
> "Elles se sont parlé."
>
> "Elles se sont lavé les mains."

The pronoun's role differs. Preserve the construction instead of adding agreement solely because "se" is present.

Distinguish the present participle from a verbal adjective where relevant: the former does not behave like an ordinary agreeing adjective. Homophonous or similar-looking forms can have different spellings and functions.

> Verbal adjective: "des exercices fatigants"
>
> Present participle with a complement: "des exercices fatiguant les élèves"

Established exceptions and accepted orthographic conventions can affect particular constructions. Use an accurate simpler sentence where it serves the prose, but do not change meaning or agency to avoid an agreement problem.

### Place adjectives according to meaning

French adjective position is partly lexical and can change meaning. It is not a choice between universally correct pre-nominal and post-nominal placement.

> "un grand homme"
>
> "un homme grand"

The first can express stature in an evaluative sense; the second describes height. Preserve the intended distinction.

Nationality, classification, and many descriptive adjectives normally follow their noun. Familiar adjectives can have established pre-nominal uses. Do not transfer an English sequence of several pre-nominal adjectives unchanged.

Unpack an overloaded attribute through a relative clause, phrase, or separate sentence. Choose the form according to emphasis rather than making ordinary description uniformly ornate.

### Choose relative pronouns by their function

Use the relative pronoun appropriate to its grammatical role, not the source word's appearance.

> "le dossier qui manque"
>
> "le dossier que j'ai envoyé"
>
> "le dossier dont nous avons parlé"
>
> "le lieu où nous travaillons"

"Qui" can be a subject whether its antecedent is a person or a thing. "Que" can be a direct object. "Dont" supplies an appropriate "de" relationship; it is not a universal formal substitute for "que". Other prepositional constructions may require forms such as "avec qui", "sur lequel", or "à laquelle".

Avoid duplicating a complement already carried by the relative pronoun. "Le dossier dont j'ai besoin" does not need an additional possessive or "de" phrase repeating the same dependency.

English relative-pronoun omission does not transfer. Keep distinctions among reference to a noun, a proposition, a place, or a time. Choose "ce qui", "ce que", or "ce dont" according to the role required.

### Preserve verb government and parallel constructions

Use the preposition and complement required by the French expression: "attendre quelque chose", "répondre à quelqu'un", "dépendre de", "se souvenir de". Do not copy source-language prepositions or reflexivity.

Two coordinated verbs can share a complement only where their constructions permit it. "Nous aidons et répondons aux usagers" combines incompatible government. "Nous aidons les usagers et répondons à leurs questions" states grammatical relationships when those are the intended actions.

Repeat a necessary preposition, article, pronoun, or verb instead of making the reader reconstruct an impossible shared form. Preserve grammatical parallelism without inventing new acts.

### Keep negation, restriction, and polarity distinct

Use "ne ... pas", "ne ... plus", "ne ... jamais", and related patterns according to the intended claim. "Ne ... que" normally restricts rather than negates: "Elle ne lit que ce dossier" means that she reads only that file.

Do not mechanically add "pas" to every negative-looking expression. "Personne n'est venu" and "Je n'ai rien trouvé" do not require it. Additional negation can change meaning.

Conversational omission of "ne" may suit speech or an established informal voice. Edited formal prose normally retains it. Do not remove it everywhere to imitate a native speaker.

An expletive "ne" can appear without negative meaning, for example in certain formal clauses after fear or "avant que". "Je crains qu'il ne parte" can mean that his leaving is feared; it does not mean he will not leave. Omit optional expletive usage when a simpler construction suits the register, without changing the polarity.

Clarify potentially consequential distinctions such as "plus" meaning an additional amount versus no longer. Context, construction, or explicit wording should resolve ambiguity.

### Choose mood according to construction and meaning

The subjunctive is not an ornamental formal version of the indicative or a universal marker of uncertainty. It follows particular constructions and meanings.

> "Je sais qu'elle vient."
>
> "Je souhaite qu'elle vienne."
>
> "Nous partons avant qu'elle arrive."

Use the required pattern with expressions of purpose, wish, concession, condition, or temporal relationship. A construction such as "de sorte que" can introduce either an intended outcome or a realized consequence, affecting mood.

For ordinary edited factual prose, use the established indicative construction after "après que" where it describes an accomplished event. Recognize that actual usage and reference traditions differ on some constructions; do not classify every attested variant as failed translation.

In an ordinary conditional pattern, "si" plus an imperfect can pair with a conditional consequence: "Si le fichier était disponible, nous l'ouvririons." Do not transfer a source conditional into both clauses mechanically. Indirect questions and other uses of "si" require their own analysis.

Preserve the evidential or temporal meaning of a conditional as explained under evidence. A grammatical mood choice must not strengthen or weaken the claim.

### Keep the small words that carry grammar

Retain articles, verbs, conjunctions, and prepositions where they make relationships intelligible. "Supprimez le fichier de sauvegarde" suits an instruction; "Supprimer le fichier de sauvegarde" suits a task label.

Avoid English-shaped notes missing required determiner or subject forms. Keep "que" where it introduces a required clause; do not omit it simply because English can omit a corresponding connector.

Repeat small grammatical words where a coordination would otherwise group the wrong objects. An intelligible sentence is more concise in practice than one requiring reconstruction.

### Use grammatically complete prose by default

Use complete sentences in ordinary explanation. Arrows, missing verbs, and compressed noun strings belong where the medium supports recoverable shorthand.

> Compressed: "Date invalide → arrêt, aucun enregistrement."
>
> Explanatory: "Si la date est invalide, le programme s'arrête sans rien enregistrer."

Labels, headings, status messages, dialogue, and specific literary effects can use fragments. That exception does not justify a whole explanation made of dramatic fragments.

### Preserve tense, aspect, and temporal reference

Choose tense according to the temporal frame and meaning. English perfect, progressive, and future constructions do not map mechanically onto French forms.

The imperfect can describe a continuing situation, habit, or background in a past frame; a compound past can present a completed event. Neither form is simply a decoration chosen for variation.

The present can express a continuing situation: "J'habite ici depuis trois ans". A completed-past form may suggest that it ended. Preserve the difference among ongoing duration, elapsed time, and a future interval.

"Être en train de" emphasizes an activity in progress. "Je lis" can already describe current reading. Do not insert a progressive expression for every English progressive or invent a habitual meaning by removing one where duration matters.

A present form can express a scheduled event: "Le cours commence demain". Future, near-future, and conditional forms can add prediction, intention, temporal perspective, or stance; they are not interchangeable.

Use an earlier-past form to orient prior events where necessary, without repeatedly marking an already established frame. Passé simple has a literary and historical narrative role, not a general requirement for polished French. Narrative applications belong under storytelling.

## Vocabulary and expression

### Prefer the familiar word that says the right thing

Use the ordinary expression where it says the right thing: "utiliser" rather than "faire usage de", "aider" rather than "apporter une assistance" where no distinction is lost, and "si" rather than "dans l'hypothèse où" for an ordinary condition.

Precision can require a technical, legal, historical, or literary word. Familiarity alone does not determine accuracy, and rarity is not a virtue.

A shorter word is not automatically an equivalent. "Hôpital" and "centre hospitalier" can have institutional distinctions; "envisager" does not always preserve the commitment expressed by intending to do something. Choose economy without weakening or broadening meaning.

### Replace inflated wording with the actual meaning

Elevated defaults make unrelated subjects sound alike. State the action, condition, result, or relationship instead of asserting importance.

| Inflated or vague use | More direct French treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "jouer un rôle crucial" | State what depends on it or what it changes. |
| "revêtir une importance capitale" | Name the requirement or consequence. |
| "offrir un éclairage approfondi" | State what is explained or understood. |
| "libérer tout le potentiel" | Name the capability or supported improvement. |
| "propulser vers de nouveaux sommets" | Describe the actual change. |
| "transformer durablement" | Specify the transformation and duration where established. |
| "holistique" | Name the included parts and their relationships. |
| "fluide", "sans friction" | Describe the transition or absence of interruption. |
| "révolutionnaire", "novateur" | State what is new and relevant without declaring historical significance. |
| "sur mesure" | State the requirement addressed. |
| "riche et foisonnant" | Name the relevant distinctions or activity. |
| "un paysage en constante évolution" | Identify the field and actual changes. |
| "créer des synergies" | Explain what cooperation enables or saves. |
| "souligner avec force" | State the supported conclusion and its bounds. |
| "témoigner de" | Identify the evidence and what it supports. |
| "s'inscrire dans une dynamique" | State the relation, development, or sequence. |

These are not prohibited strings. A literal friction problem, a defined long-term effect, or a real evidential relationship can require the term. Empty elevation is the defect; replacing it with a grander synonym leaves that defect intact.

### Say "est" or "a" when that is the relationship

Use direct identification and possession. "Ce programme est un analyseur" is clearer than an inflated account of what it "se veut" or "s'impose comme" when simple classification is intended.

Keep "sert à", "dispose de", or another construction where the relationship matters. "La pièce sert aussi d'archive" describes a use; "La maison a trois entrées" normally suffices for possession.

Do not force every predicate into a striking action verb. Classification, state, existence, and possession are legitimate meanings.

### Remove filler without removing meaning

| Padded expression | Direct treatment where equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| "en raison du fait que" | "parce que" |
| "dans le but de" | "pour" |
| "dans le cas où" | "si", with the appropriate construction |
| "en ce qui concerne" | Name the topic or relationship directly where possible. |
| "au niveau de" | Use the precise relation when no level is meant. |
| "il est important de noter que" | State the relevant fact and its consequence. |
| "il convient de rappeler que" | Give the necessary reminder without ceremonial introduction. |
| "à ce stade, force est de constater que" | State the supported observation. |
| "en conclusion, nous pouvons affirmer que" | Give a necessary synthesis or stop. |
| "éventuellement peut-être" | Retain the qualification the claim needs. |
| "dans une certaine mesure" | Specify the respect in which the claim is limited. |
| "en principe", "généralement" | Keep an actual distinction between a rule and exceptions. |
| "véritablement", "réellement", "très" | Retain a correction, contrast, degree, or voice function. |

Do not turn a qualified statement into an absolute one. "En principe autorisé" may leave room for relevant exceptions. Explain consequential exceptions rather than concealing them behind a vague qualifier.

Replacing "un très bon point" with "une observation remarquable" adds no substance. Explain relevance where needed.

### Keep terminology stable

Use the same name for an entity, operation, or defined concept. Repetition of an accurate noun is clearer than approximate synonym cycling.

Switching among "demande", "requête", "dossier", and "signalement" can imply system distinctions that do not exist. A character need not become "le protagoniste", "le héros", and "la figure centrale" merely to avoid a name.

Vary construction when phrasing becomes monotonous, not technical meaning. Explain real regional or institutional differences instead of disguising them through elegant variation.

### Choose idiomatic combinations

Use established French combinations: "prendre une décision", "poser une question", "déposer une demande", "assumer une responsabilité".

An individually accurate word can form an unnatural phrase. Express the relationship in French rather than matching source words one by one.

Preserve the particular act. "Reconnaître", "admettre", "constater", and "comprendre" are not freely interchangeable. Neither are "répondre à", "traiter", and "aborder" merely because one broad source verb can cover them.

Pay attention to familiar prepositional expressions. A source preposition is not sufficient evidence for the French construction.

### Make noun relationships explicit

French commonly expresses relationships through prepositions, adjectives, and established compounds rather than an English sequence of unmarked noun modifiers.

> Compressed: "outil analyse données clients"
>
> Clearer for one meaning: "outil d'analyse des données des clients"

The expanded form identifies one grouping; another intended relationship needs other wording. Do not force Germanic joining or English spaces into a French noun phrase.

A short established term can be useful. Long chains of "de" can still conceal which item belongs to or acts on which. Recast with a verb or clause where the hierarchy is difficult.

Distinguish an object, its source, its owner, and its purpose. A nominal label should identify the right relation, not merely combine the available topic words.

### Prefer direct verbs without banning nominal expressions

"Nous examinons la demande" often improves "Nous procédons à l'examen de la demande". "L'équipe a décidé" can be more direct than an extended nominal account.

An established noun-verb expression may carry a useful distinction. "Prendre connaissance" does not always mean the same thing as knowing, and "mettre à disposition" describes an action different from being available.

Keep an abstract noun where the concept itself is the subject. "L'authentification a échoué" can name the exact event. It need not become an unspecified person's failure.

Remove chains hiding dependencies. "Après l'achèvement de la réalisation du contrôle" usually needs a direct account of what finishes before what begins. Expose the relationship instead of merely reducing syllables.

### Choose verbs that preserve the actual action

A precise verb can replace a weak verb plus modifier when it describes the same action. A particular shuffling gait may justify "traînait les pieds"; not every slow walk does. "Chuchota" adds a manner of speech not present in every "dit doucement".

Keep adverbs expressing scope, frequency, degree, sequence, or uncertainty. "Échoue souvent" differs from "échoue". An ordinary accurate phrase can be better than a conspicuous verb.

Do not intensify action solely to remove an adverb. A reader needs the actual movement or speech, not an editorial performance of forcefulness.

### Handle false friends, calques, and borrowings by meaning

Use the French expression for the intended concept rather than the nearest-looking word.

| French expression | Meaning to preserve |
| --- | --- |
| "actuellement" | At the present time, not a general assertion of actuality. |
| "éventuellement" | A possibility or contingency, not an eventual outcome. |
| "sensible" | Sensitive in relevant usage, not automatically reasonable. |
| "assister à" | Attend an event; helping someone uses a different construction. |
| "librairie" | A bookselling establishment, not an automatic equivalent of a library. |
| "délai" | An interval, time allowed, or waiting period; the context determines whether delay or lateness is involved. |
| "supporter" | Bear, tolerate, or support in particular established senses; not every act of assistance. |
| "contrôler" | Check, inspect, or exercise control according to context; not one universal operation. |

Avoid syntactic calques that substitute translated words while retaining an unfamiliar relationship.

> Awkward calque: "Nous sommes sous l'impression que le problème est résolu."
>
> Natural: "Nous avons l'impression que le problème est résolu."

Do not turn an impression into a fact while repairing the expression.

Borrowings can be established, useful, or necessary to the readers. An exact product name, specialist term, or interface label may need to remain. Unexplained managerial borrowings can conceal ordinary meanings such as responsibility, effect, or agreement.

Regional usage matters. An accepted loan in one francophone setting may be avoided in another. Do not declare a word invalid solely because a different institution recommends an alternative, and do not assume that every fashionable borrowing is useful.

Use established French morphology where appropriate without changing names or identifiers. Avoid alternating a borrowing and its French equivalent as though they named different concepts.

### Use discourse markers for a real interpersonal effect

Conversational markers such as "bon", "ben", "enfin", "alors", "quand même", and "quoi" can express hesitation, correction, insistence, concession, or the organization of a spoken turn.

They are not interchangeable filler or automatic signs of native speech. "Enfin" can revise a statement rather than mark a final step; "quand même" can express concession or insistence. Preserve the actual stance.

Use these forms where a speaker and relationship justify them. Do not seed formal explanations with colloquial markers to simulate spontaneity. The same expression may be a logical connector in one sentence and an interpersonal marker in another.

Mitigators such as "juste", "un petit", and "simplement" can minimize a request's apparent burden. Avoid them when they trivialize real work or an error's consequence.

### Use literal meaning and purposeful imagery

In explanation, name the operation, relation, responsibility, or goal. Grand metaphors can obscure an ordinary requirement.

| Vague metaphor or abstraction | More direct French treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "poser les jalons" | Name the preparatory action and its consequence. |
| "actionner un levier" | Name the parameter, intervention, or responsibility. |
| "servir de boussole" | State the goal or decision criterion. |
| "jeter les bases" | Identify the prerequisite or supporting work. |
| "bâtir un écosystème" | Identify the interacting services, organizations, or conditions. |
| "créer des passerelles" | Explain what is connected and how. |
| "naviguer dans la complexité" | Name the choices and constraints. |
| "enclencher un cercle vertueux" | Explain the repeated process and reinforcing effect. |
| "ouvrir une nouvelle dimension" | State the new capability or implication. |
| "un enjeu" | Name what is at stake where the term merely announces importance. |
| "un changement de paradigme" | Explain the changed assumptions or method. |
| "un catalyseur" | State the process accelerated and the evidence for that role. |
| "une mosaïque d'expériences" | Identify the experiences and relevant relationships. |
| "un point d'ancrage" | Name the reference, support, or dependency. |

Literal and established technical meanings remain available. An actual ecosystem, chemical catalyst, mathematical dimension, bridge, or mosaic can require the term.

"Enjeu" and "levier" can name real stakes or interventions; they do not need removal merely because inflated prose also uses them. Explain the actual relationship when the metaphor stands in for missing thought.

In narrative, an image can create an experience literal summary cannot. It should belong to the viewpoint, world, and moment rather than supply reusable atmosphere.

### Do not assign unsupported intentions

Describe animal behavior, system operation, or a process's effect without inventing thoughts or motives. An observed action does not establish an imagined declaration such as "C'est à moi".

Technical shorthand such as "Le serveur rejette la requête" describes behavior. A product that "comprend vos besoins" needs a more precise account of the actual capability.

Literary personification can express a particular perception without asserting literal intention. Keep that purpose distinct from factual explanation.

### Express enthusiasm proportionately

Let the material establish interest. Avoid superlative chains, ceremonial praise, exclamation marks, flattering openings, and invitations to marvel together.

A measured voice can express curiosity, pleasure, anger, grief, or conviction. It need not be emotionally absent.

"C'est vraiment impressionnant" does not become evidence as "C'est absolument remarquable". State the supported achievement or keep a relevant personal judgment recognizable as a judgment.

## Rhythm and coherence

### Vary sentence movement for meaning

Short sentences can establish a point or mark a turn. Longer sentences can sustain connected explanation, condition, perception, or action. Choose length according to the thought rather than numerical alternation.

French can vary openings, subordination, presentation, and emphasis. Use those resources to develop meaning, not as a fixed cycle of structures.

Consider where the main assertion becomes available and where emphasis lands. Written rhythm does not require transcribing hesitation or adding conversational markers everywhere.

### Avoid both monotony and artificial variety

Repeated "Il est ...", "Cela montre ...", and "Dans ce contexte ..." may conceal differences among claims. Replacing them with equally empty introductions changes little.

> Mechanical: "Je pense que cette idée est utile. Je pense qu'il faut l'essayer. Je pense qu'elle fonctionnera."
>
> More direct: "Cette idée me paraît utile. Il faut l'essayer. Je m'attends à ce qu'elle fonctionne."

The revision retains judgment and expectation rather than declaring success. Variation does not authorize stronger certainty.

Avoid both nested subordinate chains and strings of abrupt fragments. Link, subordinate, or separate according to meaning. A repeated construction can serve dialogue, instruction, or rhetoric.

Do not add rare vocabulary, mistakes, random digressions, or unusual syntax to reduce predictability. Smoothness and irregularity are not independent standards of quality.

### Let paragraph length follow the material

Give a difficult explanation enough space and an important narrative consequence enough development. Short paragraphs can mark genuine turns; using one for every sentence weakens the effect.

Breaks can mark changes in thought, speaker, time, scene, or attention. Uniform lengths are not a quality standard, and constant variation is not one either.

Do not stretch or compress French paragraphs to match an English source's word count. Preserve their function and relevant information. Grammatical differences make direct word-count matching unreliable as a measure of fidelity or readability.

## Punctuation and presentation

### Use commas to clarify French clause structure

Use periods and commas for ordinary separation. Punctuation should make grammatical and meaningful boundaries visible rather than reproduce the pauses or marks of an English source.

Do not place a comma routinely between a subject and its verb or a verb and its required complement. "L'équipe confirme que le dossier est complet" normally needs none before "que".

An initial dependent clause or substantial framing phrase can take a separating comma: "Quand le dossier sera complet, nous l'examinerons." A short tightly integrated phrase need not follow an identical pattern.

Set off an inserted aside with the punctuation its structure requires, including a closing comma where needed. Do not remove one half while editing a sentence.

French can juxtapose closely related clauses with commas in appropriate constructions. Avoid an undifferentiated chain where periods, conjunctions, or a purposeful semicolon would make the relationships clearer.

Do not add an English serial comma mechanically before the last "et" or "ou" in every list. A comma can be necessary for clarity or to close another construction; decide from the actual sentence.

### Distinguish restrictive and explanatory relatives

A relative clause can identify the subset intended or add information about an already identified referent. Commas can change that relationship.

> "Les employés qui travaillent le vendredi recevront un message."
>
> "Les employés, qui travaillent le vendredi, recevront un message."

The first concerns the employees working on Friday. The second presents Friday work as additional information about the employees already identified. Do not insert or delete the commas merely for rhythm.

Do not inherit German punctuation around every relative clause or English rules tied to a particular relative word. French "qui" and "que" can occur in different informational functions.

If a consequential distinction remains difficult, recast it explicitly. Punctuation should not be the only clue to a preventable ambiguity about who receives an instruction or benefit.

### Keep sentence punctuation restrained

Do not use em dashes, en dashes, or substitute hyphens as sentence separators. Integrate short additions grammatically, give them separate sentences, or use parentheses for genuinely supplementary information.

Parentheses can hold a brief aside, abbreviation, or necessary notation. Do not hide an essential condition where readers are encouraged to skip it. Repeated parenthetical commentary can fracture a direct account.

A semicolon can link closely related clauses or separate complex list items where it clarifies structure. It is not an ornamental mark of sophistication or a substitute dash. Prefer a period when no special connection needs marking.

Use question and exclamation marks for their communicative functions. Avoid repeated rhetorical questions, multiple exclamation marks, and ellipses that manufacture suspense.

Preserve exact punctuation in faithful quotations, identifiers, commands, and formal notation. Lexical and grammatical hyphens, minus signs, and conventional ranges are not sentence separators.

### Give colons an actual introductory function

A colon can introduce a list, example, quotation, explanation, or elaboration. The preceding words should establish what follows. Do not use it automatically after every condition, heading, or bold label.

> Awkward: "Si vous modifiez les paramètres : Redémarrez le service."
>
> Clear: "Redémarrez le service après avoir modifié les paramètres."

French ordinarily continues with a lowercase letter after a colon when introducing an explanation. Proper names and full-sentence direct quotations retain their appropriate capitals; a particular formal layout may have additional conventions.

> "Le message est clair : le fichier manque."
>
> Elle a dit : "Le fichier manque."

Keep colons inside exact paths, identifiers, and required time notation intact. Their technical role differs from an editorial transition.

### Use straight quotation marks

Use straight double quotation marks for authored French examples, dialogue, and quoted expressions. Use straight single quotes for a quotation inside one of those passages. This is a house convention rather than a claim that French guillemets are incorrect.

Preserve faithful source quotations and required publication notation. When a publication requires guillemets or a particular dialogue convention, its necessary notation can remain within that scope.

Distinguish punctuation belonging to the quoted words from punctuation belonging to the surrounding sentence.

> Elle a répondu : "Je viendrai demain."
>
> "Je viendrai demain", a-t-elle répondu.
>
> "Viendras-tu demain ?" a-t-elle demandé.

A reporting clause after a question or exclamation does not require a borrowed English or German comma pattern. Use a coherent French publication convention.

Do not put quotation marks around ordinary words for decorative emphasis or insinuated doubt. If skepticism matters, explain its basis.

### Apply spacing appropriate to the locale and medium

French typographic spacing differs from English and can vary across francophone contexts. Keep punctuation attached to the word or passage it belongs to rather than stranded at a line break.

Use nonbreaking spacing before a prose colon and between a number and a relevant unit where the medium supports it. Do not insert spaces around apostrophes or inside grammatical hyphenations.

In French publishing conventions used in France and some other contexts, semicolons, question marks, and exclamation marks commonly take a preceding nonbreaking or fine nonbreaking space. Quebec conventions commonly omit that space, while permitting fine spacing in suitable composition.

Follow the intended locale or publication consistently. Do not present either system as the only native French typography. Unspecified illustrative examples can use a consistent spaced convention without requiring it for every audience.

Straight quotes take no padding immediately inside them under the house convention. If required guillemets are used, apply their conventional internal spacing rather than treating them as straight quotes.

Use a reasonable plain-text fallback when special spaces are unavailable. Exact code, identifiers, URLs, and machine-readable formats retain their syntax. Do not insert French prose spacing into them.

### Use French capitalization and preserve accents

Use sentence-case headings. Capitalize the first word and required proper names, not every important noun. Do not import English title case or German noun capitalization.

> Informative heading: "Quand la sauvegarde expire"
>
> Vague heading: "Points importants à considérer"

Retain accents, the cedilla, and other required marks on capitals: "État", "À propos", "ÇA". Removing marks is not a modern style choice when it changes correct spelling.

Weekdays and months normally remain lowercase: "lundi", "avril". Language names and nationality adjectives normally do as well: "le français"; "une équipe française". Nouns naming inhabitants have their own capitalization conventions: "les Français". Preserve the distinction rather than using one blanket rule.

Titles of works, official institutions, and established names may follow specific conventions. Keep their exact form rather than applying the heading rule indiscriminately.

Use a logical heading hierarchy. Headings should help navigation rather than decorate every short paragraph. A heading normally needs no final period.

### Preserve grammatical apostrophes and hyphens

Apostrophes carrying the elisions described under syntax remain part of the word form. Keep them without surrounding spaces; removing one is not a punctuation simplification.

Use hyphens where French grammar requires them.

> "Envoyez-le-moi."
>
> "A-t-elle répondu ?"
>
> "Vas-y."

Distinguish these from sentence dashes. Do not remove an imperative's hyphens during a punctuation simplification.

Lexical compounds have established joining, spacing, hyphenation, and plural patterns. Follow the chosen accepted spelling convention rather than joining every component as in Germanic compounding or separating every word as in English.

Keep exact names, identifiers, and formal notation. An apostrophe or hyphen in a technical value is data rather than an invitation to impose prose typography.

### Present numbers, dates, and units for the intended context

Use the decimal comma in ordinary French prose: "1,5 litre". Group larger numbers according to the applicable convention, commonly with nonbreaking spaces rather than English thousands commas.

Separate a number from a unit or percent sign according to the relevant convention: "15 km", "23 °C", "20 %". Use a nonbreaking space where supported. Keep symbols and their case exact.

Make dates unambiguous: "3 avril 2026". Month names normally remain lowercase. Use appropriate ordinal notation where needed, such as "1er avril", rather than adding an English suffix.

Time presentation depends on context. "14 h 30" is natural in ordinary French prose; digital displays and exact system values may use colon notation. Preserve the relevant timezone and actual event time.

Currency symbols commonly follow amounts in French presentation, with appropriate spacing: "12,50 €". Regional naming and institutional requirements may add a currency designation.

Formatting is not conversion. Changing a separator or symbol position does not change a value. Converting currency, measurements, or timezones requires a reliable basis and relevant reference conditions.

Do not silently substitute a euro price for a dollar price, a local institution for a factual one, or a familiar measurement for a different quantity. Code, data, identifiers, and required scientific notation retain their exact syntax.

### Keep abbreviations and symbols dependable

Use established French abbreviations and symbols rather than importing every English form. Introduce unfamiliar abbreviations sufficiently for the reader, as explained under reader knowledge.

Do not pluralize or punctuate a unit symbol as an ordinary word. Keep a variable or acronym's case where it determines identity. A publication's scientific or bibliographic notation can differ from general prose.

Avoid abbreviated courtesy titles or compressed labels where they save little and make reference unclear.

### Use emphasis sparingly

Bold should help readers find an important distinction or actual interface label. Do not bold every noun, acronym, opening phrase, or repeated term.

Avoid a label followed by a sentence repeating it. "Performances : Les performances sont meilleures" supplies no useful distinction. A label should introduce new information.

Use italics or quotation marks for a real linguistic or publication function rather than habitual emphasis. Competing emphasis systems obscure the hierarchy.

### Let lists express actual structure

Use numbered lists for ordered sequences and bullets for parallel unordered items. Explain what the list represents and keep its grammar parallel.

Whole-sentence items retain sentence punctuation. Fragments and items integrated with an introductory sentence follow a coherent convention suited to their structure. Do not force short labels into sentences or omit necessary verbs from explanation.

Use paragraphs for connected argument. A list cannot replace an explanation of relationships. Tables suit comparisons and mappings.

Do not force three items, clauses, or examples onto material that naturally has another shape. Symmetry is useful only when it reflects the subject.

### Remove decorative interface and chatbot residue

Begin with useful information. Omit automatic acknowledgments, flattery, congratulations, repeated offers of help, and generic sign-offs from substantive prose.

Typical padding includes "Bien sûr !", "Excellente question !", "Vous avez tout à fait raison", "Plongeons dans le sujet", "J'espère que cela vous aide", and "N'hésitez pas à me solliciter". A genuine greeting or closing can belong to correspondence; a quotation may require the words. Function determines whether they stay.

Omit decorative emojis from headings and bullets. Preserve symbols used as data, required interface content, or the subject of discussion.

Remove commentary about preparing the answer, assurances of completeness, and self-praise about clarity. Provide the material instead of announcing the quality of its presentation.

## Style-specific conventions

Apply the general principles throughout. Each style adds requirements and narrow exceptions that perform a concrete function. A document may include explanation followed by instructions; keep each passage appropriate to its role.

Style does not authorize invented evidence, unsupported certainty, ornamental terminology, mechanical repetition, or obscure syntax. A scholarly convention needs a scholarly function; a literary device needs a narrative purpose.

### Academic

#### Formality with accessible language

Use precise, accessible French appropriate to the scholarly context. Avoid slang, promotional claims, conversational padding, forced intimacy, and ceremonial abstraction.

> Inflated: "La présente investigation se propose de mettre en lumière les interactions complexes et multidimensionnelles entre les facteurs susmentionnés."
>
> Direct: "Cette étude examine les interactions entre les facteurs mentionnés."

Retain "complexes" or "multidimensionnelles" if those properties identify actual analytical distinctions. Name the factors where the preceding text does not. A defined statistical interaction should retain its exact term rather than a looser metaphor.

Formality comes from explicit reasoning, proportionate claims, and dependable terminology. Rare words, long nominal groups, and unnecessary Latin cannot supply those qualities.

#### Disciplinary voice and researcher position

Use first person when it identifies a decision, argument, or relevant relationship to the material. "J'interprète ce passage comme une objection à cette lecture" locates an interpretation. "Nous avons exclu les réponses incomplètes" identifies agency.

First and third person do not independently guarantee objectivity. Match the particular field, form, and venue. A passive can foreground a specimen while an active sentence names a research choice.

An established scholarly "nous" may have a conventional role; make authorship and responsibility intelligible. Do not invent several researchers when one person acted. Avoid a vague "on" where readers need to identify who analyzed or interpreted the evidence.

| Kind of writing | Relevant emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| Empirical research | Separate measured observations, reported experiences, analytical decisions, and interpretations; identify consequential agency. |
| Interpretive scholarship | Ground readings in the text, record, object, or experience and explain the inferential move. |
| Theoretical argument | Define concepts, expose assumptions, develop relationships, and distinguish proposals from established findings. |
| Practitioner-facing research | Explain practical consequences, intended users, conditions, and constraints without unsupported promises. |

Where positionality affects interpretation, describe actual access, involvement, institutional role, or experience. A generic neutrality claim or personal confession does not explain that relationship. Keep participant perspectives separate from researcher interpretation.

#### Research questions and contributions

State the question precisely enough to identify the relevant phenomenon, population or material, setting, and relationship. Distinguish descriptive, comparative, predictive, causal, and exploratory purposes.

Align the contribution with the problem established. Another prevalence estimate does not answer a gap concerning a mechanism. Explain the substantive difference from the nearest relevant work instead of declaring an entire topic unexplored.

Bound "la première étude" and absence claims by the literature actually covered. "À notre connaissance" does not alone establish the scope of that knowledge. Where an absence claim matters, identify relevant scope and date.

> Overbroad: "Aucune recherche n'a étudié ce problème."
>
> More specific form: "Les études examinées estiment la fréquence dans les établissements urbains. Les données sur le mécanisme dans les établissements ruraux restent limitées."

Both claims in the alternative require support. A limitation in a review is not automatically an absence across the field. Do not invent a location or mechanism gap to make a contribution seem original.

#### Titles, abstracts, and keywords

A title identifies the subject and necessary scope. Put useful concepts ahead of empty openers such as "Étude de". Include population, setting, material, or design where that information changes expectations.

A declarative title needs the same evidential restraint as the paper. A question title should name a question actually addressed. Brevity and wordplay do not justify dropping a decisive qualification.

An abstract should stand alone and represent purpose, approach, principal findings, and contribution accurately. Include decisive sample or contextual information and limits. Report findings rather than announcing that results will be discussed.

Keep abstract and body consistent. Do not add a causal claim, numerical result, or recommendation absent from the paper. Define unfamiliar abbreviations sufficiently for independent reading.

Structured and unstructured abstracts serve different forms. Follow the required form without imposing a universal word count or sentence recipe. Remove repeated background before removing a condition determining truth.

Keywords should identify actual concepts, populations, settings, or distinctive methods using terms relevant readers would search for. Generic "recherche" and "étude" add little unless they name the subject. Align terminology across title, abstract, keywords, and main text.

#### Literature synthesis

Organize around questions, findings, methods, theoretical positions, or disagreements relevant to the argument. Use chronology where historical development matters. An author-by-author inventory is insufficient where readers need relationships.

Explain convergence, conflict, and dependence on measurements, populations, settings, or designs. Distinguish an author's argument from an empirical finding. Group references supporting one proposition and separate those supporting materially different ones.

> Inventory: "Une étude examine la participation. Une autre étude analyse également la participation. Une troisième étude aborde le même sujet."
>
> Synthesis form: "Les études constatent toutes une baisse de la participation, mais couvrent des périodes différentes. Seule l'étude longitudinale suit les mêmes personnes sur plusieurs années."

The synthesis requires those relationships to be established. Do not manufacture agreement, two opposing camps, or a common mechanism for rhetorical neatness.

Explain why a gap matters. Limited coverage of a location does not automatically justify another study there. Keep foundational work for conceptual origins and current evidence for time-sensitive claims. Age and prestige alone do not determine relevance.

#### Sections that perform distinct functions

Let article type and reasoning determine organization. Functions may occupy separate sections or be combined where the form requires.

| Section | Writing function |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction | Establish the specific problem, necessary context, uncertainty, question, and contribution without a sweeping historical opening. |
| Literature or conceptual background | Establish concepts and evidence the argument depends on and explain what remains unresolved. |
| Methods | Describe design, participants or material, measures, procedures, analysis, and consequential choices sufficiently to understand the evidence. |
| Results or findings | Present relevant findings, including negative and inconclusive ones; lead with what was found rather than an unexplained test name. |
| Discussion | Explain meaning, relation to prior work, alternatives, limits, and supported implications; separate these from proposed future work. |
| Conclusion | State the established contribution and essential bounds without new evidence or a stronger version of the finding. |

When findings and discussion are separated, keep reporting and interpretation distinguishable. Qualitative and interpretive work may integrate them more closely. A conventional label does not require concealing an explanation essential to understanding a finding.

| Form | Additional structural emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| Empirical paper | Connect question, design, findings, and interpretation; explain what the design permits. |
| Research review | Explain coverage and selection principles; distinguish systematic coverage from selective or interpretive synthesis. |
| Theoretical paper | Establish a problem in an existing account, develop reasoning, and specify what an extension explains or leaves open. |
| Case study | Supply context, selection rationale, evidence, and analytical relevance; bound transfer beyond the case. |
| Research proposal | Separate completed work and existing evidence from planned methods, expected outcomes, and possible benefits. |
| Policy brief | Present the problem, evidence, options, tradeoffs, recommendation, and relevant constraints; distinguish findings from value judgments. |

These are available forms rather than mandatory outlines or word allocations. A French paper does not need artificial dialectical symmetry where its question requires another structure.

#### Methods and findings in concrete terms

Describe what was actually done, measured, excluded, and observed. Specify relevant selection, recruitment, material, timing, instruments, missing data, and analytical choices. Do not fill unknown details with plausible conventions.

Use "répartis aléatoirement" only for actual random assignment. Separate planned work from completed procedures and observed practice from formal approval.

Define constructs operationally where needed. A self-report of confidence does not directly measure competence. An interview account differs from observing behavior. Name the distinction without dismissing a suitable method.

Distinguish prior hypotheses from explanations developed after seeing results. Describe exploratory analyses as exploratory. An unexpected result can matter without being rewritten as a prediction.

For qualitative findings, make the relationship between interpretation and material intelligible. Preserve participants' words, context, and translated terms' meaning. A quotation illustrates a theme without establishing prevalence. Counts of coded interviews need a denominator and do not automatically describe a wider population.

#### Statistical findings with substantive meaning

Report important estimates with sample information, magnitude, units, and uncertainty. Include the statistic, degrees of freedom, exact probability value, effect measure, and interval where relevant to the method and venue.

Explain substantive meaning rather than leaving symbols to carry the conclusion. Distinguish standard deviation, standard error, and an interval for an estimate. Identify adjusted estimates, comparison groups, and relevant differences between original and analytical samples.

Describe multiplicity adjustments, sensitivity analyses, and consequential departures from the stated analysis where they affect interpretation. Do not call a finding "robuste" solely because one alternative specification gives a similar result.

Report material negative, contradictory, and inconclusive findings. Do not hide them through changed outcomes, omitted subgroups, or selective emphasis on exploratory patterns.

Keep numbers, directions, labels, and qualifications consistent across prose, tables, figures, titles, and abstracts. Tables can carry comparative detail; prose should explain principal findings and meaning. Captions and notes need the sample, measures, units, abbreviations, uncertainty, and source information necessary to interpret them.

#### Claims, evidence, and limits

Tie claims to the actual design. Self-reported productivity differs from direct measurement; association differs from causation.

> Too strong: "L'enquête prouve que le télétravail augmente la productivité."
>
> Bounded, if supported: "Dans cette enquête, les personnes en télétravail ont déclaré une productivité plus élevée."

Place material limits beside the claims they affect. General evidence conventions govern attribution, uncertainty, and inferential strength. Scholarly tone does not require stacks of "peut-être", "éventuellement", and "il serait possible de suggérer".

Do not append generic limitations while leaving the body overstated. Explain the actual effect of a consequential limit on interpretation, transfer, or precision.

#### Substantive sentences and stable terminology

Each sentence should establish a fact, define a concept, explain a relationship, supply evidence, or delimit the argument. Avoid statements merely announcing importance.

Use the same name for a construct, measure, group, or method. Reduce repeated phrasing without substituting near-synonyms for defined terms.

> Empty repetition: "Les résultats sont importants. Ces observations majeures ont des implications considérables."
>
> Substantive treatment: State the actual implication and the evidence supporting it.

A fashionable conceptual label should not replace a familiar precise relationship. Define a necessary specialized distinction and maintain it.

#### Syntax that makes reasoning explicit

Keep the main proposition visible. Use subordination for actual dependencies, conditions, and concessions without burying the assertion under layers of qualification.

Use active voice to identify research decisions and passive voice to foreground methods or material. Avoid impersonal formulations that conceal who interpreted evidence.

Completed procedures and observations normally concern the past; definitions, the current argument, and continuing propositions may use the present. An enduring work may be discussed in the present while its publication history remains past.

Do not imitate English trailing participles or overload a French sentence to match a compact source-language abstract. State the inferential step explicitly.

#### Necessary formal conventions

Use the publication's citation system consistently. Author-date and numbered systems organize references differently; neither is universal for French scholarship. Keep entries, locators, and attached claims aligned.

Preserve required parentheses, brackets, abbreviations, bibliographic punctuation, statistical symbols, equations, variables, and units. Necessary notation can differ from ordinary typography or locale presentation.

These conventions do not justify optional asides everywhere, needless Latin, or inflated diction. Keep exact quoted wording distinct from surrounding house typography.

#### Correspondence and declarations

Academic correspondence identifies the substantive point in professional French. A submission letter can explain question, contribution, and relevance without praising the journal or overstating novelty.

A response to criticism should identify the issue, explain a change or reasoned disagreement, and locate the passage where useful. Distinguish completed changes from proposed work. Formulaic gratitude does not answer a methodological concern; respect does not require accepting unsupported criticism.

State contributions, funding, conflicts, ethics approval, consent, assistance, and material availability accurately when required. Describe actual activities instead of vague assurances. Do not imply approval, access, preregistration, independent review, or verification that did not occur.

Distinguish open access, restricted access, access on request, and material that cannot be shared. Give real conditions. Describe assistance by its actual use and extent without transferring responsibility for the claims.

#### Worked example

> Before: "Il convient de souligner que nos résultats démontrent incontestablement que tous les participants ont bénéficié de l'intervention, ce qui pourrait constituer une avancée révolutionnaire."
>
> Bounded alternative, for evidence supporting reported benefits only: "Des participants ont signalé des améliorations après l'intervention. L'étude ne permet pas d'établir si l'intervention a causé ces changements."

The alternative requires evidence for both the reported improvements and the inferential limit. If benefits for every participant or a causal conclusion are supported, retain that information instead of weakening it automatically.

### Technical

#### Organize around the reader's purpose

A technical document should support one main purpose. Identify substantial material serving another purpose separately, connected by a descriptive link where useful.

| Form | Reader's need | Writing requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tutorial | Learn through a successful practical experience. | State what the reader builds; supply a supported sequence, visible results, expected output, and proportionate background. |
| How-to | Complete a specific task. | Assume relevant competence; name actions and choices; keep unrelated explanation outside the sequence. |
| Reference | Find dependable facts. | State options, behavior, constraints, and errors in the system's recognizable structure. |
| Explanation | Understand a bounded subject and its reasons. | Explain design choices, context, history, alternatives, and tradeoffs apart from a task sequence. |

A tutorial can use "nous" for a genuinely shared learning sequence. Instructions address the reader or use a suitable infinitive. Reference prose describes the system; explanation can express a reasoned design judgment.

Use task titles such as "Créer une instance" and concept headings such as "Organisation de la mémoire". An explanation names a bounded subject; it need not imitate an English title formula.

#### Make instructions actionable

Imperatives and infinitives can both express instructions. Choose a form appropriate to the medium and keep the sequence coherent.

> Reader-directed instruction: "Sélectionnez le fichier."
>
> Neutral task label: "Sélectionner le fichier"
>
> Prohibition in a general notice: "Ne pas ouvrir."

Do not alternate infinitives and imperatives randomly. Keep "tu" or "vous" agreement where the chosen address appears. Use the correct negative construction and pronoun placement.

Give each instruction one principal action. Put prerequisites and warnings before the action they govern. State the common case before exceptions unless an exception must be known first.

Describe the visible result where it helps readers recognize progress: output, changed state, prompt, or relevant log entry. Do not invent a screen state or imply command success without evidence.

Avoid repeating "veuillez" or "s'il vous plaît" in every step. Do not minimize effort with "simplement", "facilement", or "il suffit de" when the action has real conditions. A supported duration may be stated separately.

Use present-tense descriptions for current behavior. Distinguish planned capability from available capability rather than promising future features inside a current reference.

#### Keep terminology and syntax dependable

Use actual operation names, symbols, files, flags, commands, and labels. Give one term one meaning. "Contrôler", "valider", "activer", and "sélectionner" should remain distinct where the system distinguishes their actions.

Explain necessary terms near first relevant use. Avoid idioms, unfamiliar borrowings, unnecessary Latin abbreviations, and figurative descriptions that obstruct interpretation.

Keep conditions near their actions and the main assertion visible. Several exclusions may need separate sentences or a meaningful list. Sentence length is a density signal, not a fixed French word limit.

Use a category noun around an identifier when necessary for agreement. Do not change the identifier itself to fit French grammar.

#### Preserve exact notation

Use explicit alternatives where a slash or "et/ou" hides grouping. "A, B ou les deux" expresses an inclusive choice where intended. An exclusive choice needs different wording.

Avoid improvised singular-plural labels such as "fichier(s)" in explanatory prose. State actual number or use wording covering the supported cases naturally.

Format code, commands, flags, and paths as code. Preserve exact case, characters, punctuation, and meaningful whitespace. Follow the programming language and project for indentation.

Keep interpolation variables, markup, placeholders, and link destinations exact. Reorder surrounding prose for French syntax. Variable values may change number, gender, article choice, or elision; use wording grammatical for the supported values rather than assuming every value behaves alike.

Use real names and paths in factual documentation. Mark illustrative values as examples. Counts and directory trees must match the described state; include their reproducible basis where it matters.

#### Make documents easy to navigate

Use informative task and concept headings with a logical hierarchy. Prefer descriptive links to "cliquez ici". Put essential context on the current page instead of requiring another link for the basic explanation.

Use French list punctuation. Avoid "etc." where it conceals an unspecified category; state whether a list is partial and what defines its scope.

Name interface controls accurately, including their language. Do not translate a displayed English label into a nonexistent French control. The explanation can remain French while an exact label retains its spelling.

#### Keep change descriptions focused

A change description or commit message should identify the concrete problem and resulting behavior. Include the condition exposing the issue and relevant evidence.

Logs, commit lists, and measurement tables support the explanation rather than replace it. Distinguish observed behavior, intended behavior, and unverified expectation.

Interface text follows its screen and action. Documentation conventions do not justify paragraphs inside buttons. "Enregistrer" names an action; "Enregistré" reports a result and may require agreement with its actual referent. Do not announce success while an action is merely scheduled or in progress.

#### Example

> Dense: "La configuration des paramètres du script de contrôle du budget d'importation est effectuée via `budget.json`. Il convient de noter que l'exécution avec `--write`, qui ajuste la valeur enregistrée au nombre actuel, ne doit intervenir que lors d'une réduction. En cas de dépassement, le contrôle échoue."
>
> Clearer: "Le script lit le budget d'importation dans `budget.json`. Si le nombre actuel dépasse ce budget, le contrôle échoue. Utilisez `--write` uniquement pour réduire le budget enregistré."

The revision preserves the file, condition, and restricted action. Retain additional conditions necessary to use the command correctly.

### Absolute

#### Directness without lost meaning

Give the useful answer in plain French. Keep the reason, condition, consequence, or necessary limitation close to it. Explain required terms through their actual effect and remove unnecessary jargon.

Directness does not require bluntness, hostility, excessive certainty, or telegraphic English-shaped notes. A complete neutral sentence can be concise.

#### Complete sentences with no padding

Omit greetings, pre-narration, decorative headings, repeated conclusions, flattering acknowledgments, and invitations to continue. Retain articles, verbs, pronouns, and other elements readers need.

> Compressed: "Jeton absent. Nouvel essai inutile."
>
> Clear for the established issue: "Il manque un jeton dans la requête. Ajoutez-en un avant de réessayer."

The alternative assumes the missing token is established and can be supplied. Preserve uncertainty where the diagnosis is only possible. A fragment can serve a real label without becoming the default for explanation.

#### Necessary explanation stays

Keep qualifications changing the answer, warnings affecting action, and distinctions between recommendations and requirements.

> Insufficient: "Supprimez le fichier."
>
> Sufficient for the stated case: "Supprimez le doublon après avoir vérifié que le fichier original s'ouvre."

The condition belongs to this recommendation. Do not invent it as a generic precaution unrelated to the actual task. Direct prose should be sufficient for the real stakes.

#### Worked example

> Before: "Bien sûr ! À un niveau global, il semblerait que nous soyons potentiellement confrontés à une problématique d'authentification susceptible d'être résolue par un renouvellement du jeton."
>
> After, if expiry is the relevant possibility: "Le jeton a peut-être expiré. Renouvelez-le et réessayez."

If expiry is established, "Le jeton a expiré" is appropriate. If renewal needs a condition or different procedure, retain it. Directness cannot turn an unspecified issue into a convenient diagnosis.

### Article

#### Concrete material and a reason to read

Lead with an artifact, observation, example, event, supported number, or specific tension. Let it establish why the subject matters. Give broader explanation after concrete material where that order helps.

Avoid sweeping declarations about the modern world, irrelevant biography, and announcements that the article will explore its topic. A quiet opening can still provide a reason to continue.

A French article may be restrained, skeptical, reflective, conversational, or expansive. Do not impose a generic national personality or translate a promotional voice word for word.

#### Headlines, standfirsts, and news leads

A headline should reflect the story's evidence and certainty. Preserve consequential limits of population, timing, and scope. A question must not insinuate an unsupported allegation.

Brevity and search-friendly wording do not justify exaggeration. Headline compression must not turn a proposal into a decision or an association into a cause.

A standfirst adds development, context, or stakes rather than repeating the headline in synonyms. Keep the scope consistent across headline, standfirst, and body.

A news lead establishes the consequential development and essential people, place, or timing. Prefer the outcome to the fact that a routine meeting occurred.

> Meeting-centered: "Le conseil a discuté des tarifs mardi."
>
> Outcome-centered, if established: "Le conseil a décidé mardi de porter le prix du billet de bus de 2 à 2,50 euros en janvier."

The alternative adds a decision, amounts, and effective date that must be known. Include the year where needed. It is not a permissible paraphrase of a meeting with an unknown outcome.

A feature can open with a scene or individual experience. Supply a timely explanation of the broader subject and stakes. Delayed explanation should create meaningful interest rather than conceal a weak premise.

#### A coherent line of development

Give each section a distinct contribution. Develop evidence, examples, implications, and relevant objections without forcing symmetrical sections.

Keep the author's actual position recognizable. Voice need not become a sequence of generic commentary formulas.

A sustained thought may require a longer sentence or paragraph. Expansion should add explanation or experience rather than another paraphrase of the thesis. Use transitions for actual relationships.

#### Credibility and proportion

Support factual claims with available evidence. Distinguish reported cases, illustration, opinion, and interpretation. Do not invent customer experience, credentials, results, conversations, or personal vulnerability.

Attribute allegations and disputed accounts. Separate what records establish, what a source says, what the subject disputes, and what remains unknown. Maintain attribution boundaries and appropriate reporting stance as described under evidence.

Do not use a conditional to insinuate unsupported allegations while avoiding responsibility for stating them. Nor should a neutral account acquire skepticism solely because a different reporting verb sounds less repetitive.

Represent available responses substantively. Do not imply refusal to comment unless that occurred or treat silence as evidence of guilt.

#### Data reporting with context and human consequences

Lead with the finding answering the reporting question. Give meaningful historical, geographic, or other comparisons to explain scale, using comparable definitions and periods.

Explain whether a change concerns the phenomenon or its recording. Administrative records measure what the collection system captures, not necessarily every event.

Connect patterns to affected people where supported. Individual accounts illustrate experience without establishing prevalence. Make their role and selection intelligible, especially when an unusual case supplies the opening.

Keep data and testimony distinguishable. Expert interpretation can explain a result without replacing it or removing uncertainty. An unresolved discrepancy can remain part of the story.

Distinguish trends from forecasts and possible actions from demonstrated remedies. Striking figures do not make a future inevitable.

#### Public explanations of methodology

Explain how the finding was produced and where its limits lie. Identify the data publisher and source, relevant release or access date, coverage period, population, geography, and consequential definitions.

Explain important exclusions, missing records, transformations, adjustments, and comparison choices in plain French. A count of reported incidents does not automatically count every incident.

Describe material sensitivity to definitions and known limitations. State what verification actually occurred, and explain access to data, code, or supporting material with real restrictions.

A concise passage can link to fuller detail without hiding a limitation that changes the central finding. Methodology is a reporting function, not a catalog of internal operations.

#### Captions, chart text, and accessible descriptions

Captions identify the relevant person, place, event, or evidence and necessary context. Distinguish when an image was made from when an event happened. Identify archival, illustrative, or reconstructed material where readers might otherwise mistake it for a record of the event.

Chart titles, labels, annotations, and notes need quantities, units, periods, populations, uncertainty, and source information. Distinguish counts from rates and adjusted from unadjusted values.

A chart title cannot assert a cause the display does not establish. Keep number presentation, labels, and comparison directions consistent with prose.

Alternative text conveys relevant information or function in context. For a chart, give the principal relationship and essential uncertainty rather than only "Graphique des résultats". Supply a fuller description or accessible table where readers need detail.

Avoid redundant visual inventories and unsupported interpretation. Give required credits accurately.

#### Corrections and substantive updates

Identify the material error and state corrected information plainly. Explain any change to the central finding. Do not disguise an error as "précisé" or "mis à jour".

Distinguish correction from new information about an evolving event. Give sufficient timing and context without repeating misleading or harmful detail beyond what the correction needs.

#### Characteristic formulas to remove

Remove empty openings such as "Dans un monde en constante évolution", automatic transformation claims, unsupported "révolutionnaire" judgments, and bridges such as "Pourquoi est-ce important ?" where the consequence can be stated directly.

Avoid fake vulnerability arcs, biography padding, staged revelations, and closing questions added only for engagement. Smoother French cannot supply missing substance.

A genuine question or relevant personal experience may belong. The problem is fabricated connection or unearned importance, not first person itself.

#### Guides, essays, opinion, and newsletters

| Form | Writing requirements |
| --- | --- |
| Technical guide | Open with the practical result; use relevant commands, code, screenshots, or concrete output; end with a useful consequence. |
| Essay | Develop a connected inquiry or argument from a specific observation, tension, or genuine contradiction; let progression earn the conclusion. |
| Opinion | State the position and answer to evidence; represent serious alternatives fairly rather than invent weak opponents. |
| Newsletter | Make the opening screen useful; keep diary material when it serves the piece and labels when they support navigation. |

Narrative techniques do not authorize invented reporting. Reported anecdotes must remain factual.

#### Worked example

> Before: "Dans le paysage professionnel d'aujourd'hui, notre outil révolutionnaire ouvre de nouvelles perspectives aux équipes débordées. Pourquoi est-ce essentiel ? La réponse est simple !"
>
> Alternative for an illustrative product with the stated behavior: "L'outil enregistre chaque brouillon automatiquement. Si l'envoi échoue, le travail de l'équipe est conservé."

Both capability and consequence must be established. Unknown behavior cannot be supplied through stylistic improvement.

### Storytelling

#### Particular experience rather than generic atmosphere

Make the scene belong to these characters, in this world, under these conditions. Attention, knowledge, desire, fear, experience, and immediate purpose shape what a viewpoint character notices.

One relevant detail can contribute more than an inventory of everything visible. Details can be visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, or conceptual. Do not add smells, bodily sensations, or unusual defects merely to create vividness.

> Generic: "La pièce était belle et pleine de souvenirs."
>
> Illustrative scene: "Quelqu'un avait passé le chiffon autour du rectangle plus clair où se trouvait autrefois la photo de son père."

The alternative invents a particular situation. It is fictional specificity rather than faithful paraphrase. Details should belong to the authorized story.

#### Voice, viewpoint, and distance

Keep narrator register and perspective intelligible. A viewpoint or distance shift needs a function and sufficient orientation.

Distinguish narrator knowledge from character knowledge. Do not give a character information they have not acquired. In close viewpoint, filter description and comparison through what the character could notice and understand.

Vocabulary and syntax can reflect occupation, education, age, relationships, and emotional state without turning those influences into caricature. Grammatical deviation is not a shorthand for intelligence.

Free indirect discourse can place character judgment inside third-person narration.

> "Elle repoussa la lettre. Allait-elle encore lui faire confiance ?"

Keep character uncertainty or prejudice distinguishable from established narrator fact. A native French interior voice need not copy the English source's sentence shape.

#### Make narrative elements work together

| Concern | Writing implication |
| --- | --- |
| Meaning | Let choices, consequences, relationships, and contrasts carry thematic questions. |
| Character change | Make desires, beliefs, pressures, decisions, and effects intelligible where an arc depends on them. |
| Plot | Establish consequential relationships among events instead of a list of incidents. |
| Mystery | Shape knowledge, suspicion, misunderstanding, and reinterpretation. |
| World | Let physical and social conditions constrain and enable action. |
| Relationships | Give bonds, status, obligations, and conflict particular stakes. |
| Prose | Make movement, detail, voice, and devices serve the moment. |

A setting detail can reveal a rule and pressure a relationship. Dialogue can alter an alliance and disclose a motive. Do not make every sentence carry the same load.

A flaw, crisis, and transformation can structure an arc. Stasis, refusal, tragic failure, ensemble change, and developing perception are also available. Do not impose one corrective lesson on every character.

#### Causality, cost, and consequence

Make choices matter where agency matters. Establish what enables an action, its immediate effect, and consequences altering later choices.

Coherence does not require a single-track plot or a protagonist controlling every outcome. Chance, institutions, other characters, and external events can matter. Avoid arbitrary convenience and artificial tidiness.

Keep world rules consistent. A power, technology, or institution should not gain an unprepared capability solely to resolve the current problem. Reader understanding should support the credibility of a conflict-solving capability.

Limits can create stronger choices than extra powers. Develop established possibilities before adding new ones. Maintain geography, travel time, seasons, physical details, relationships, and knowledge. Changed behavior needs a basis where the story depends on it.

#### Information, suspense, and revelation

Supply enough orientation to understand the scene while withholding information that creates a purposeful question. Productive uncertainty differs from confusion about actor, location, or physical action.

Mystery may concern events, motives, relationships, world rules, or outcomes. Plant consequential details fairly. A revelation should change understanding where that effect is promised.

Flashbacks, flash-forwards, delayed disclosure, and multiple timelines are available tools. Temporal complexity is not obligatory; linearity is not a defect.

Endings may resolve, partly resolve, or leave questions open. A chapter can create forward interest without an automatic cliffhanger. A completed story can close its questions without resolving every relationship neatly.

Let consequences carry meaning. Outcomes can retain ambiguity, acceptance, action, or unresolved cost. Do not append a universal lesson to an already intelligible scene.

#### Emotion, behavior, and subtext

Use labels, action, silence, dialogue, sensation, or thought as needed. "Elle avait peur" can suffice. Repeated tight chests, held breaths, cold blood, and trembling hands can be formulaic too.

| General statement | Particular fictional treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "Elle se mit en colère." | "En ouvrant la main, elle découvrit la marque de son ongle dans sa paume." |
| "Il était terrifié." | "Sa main tremblait trop pour qu'il puisse introduire la clé dans la serrure." |
| "Le départ la rendait triste." | "Elle remit les coussins en place une dernière fois, puis ferma la porte sans se retourner." |

Each alternative adds behavior and implications requiring a place in the scene. They are not exact translations of every emotion. Self-awareness, pain, reluctance, and practical difficulty are fictional choices.

Avoid explaining subtext immediately after readers can infer it. Characters may evade, conceal, misunderstand, or speak directly. Not every emotional exchange needs a hidden second meaning.

#### Dialogue with identifiable speakers

Distinguish speakers through vocabulary, syntax, rhythm, attention, and intention. A line belongs to a person in a relationship rather than a generic witty narrator.

Dialogue can advance action, reveal character, establish a world, create humor, and express disagreement. Avoid turning every exchange into philosophy, a miniature speech, or a statement of theme.

Ordinary tags such as "dit-elle" and "demanda-t-il" can identify speech unobtrusively. Replacing every one with an expressive verb may alter the action and create noise. A relevant action beat can reveal hesitation or intent.

Keep speakers intelligible. Every line need not be identifiable without attribution. Do not add an action after every utterance merely to avoid a repeated tag.

Address, negative forms, discourse markers, and vocabulary carry relationships. A shift from "vous" to "tu" can matter. Use colloquial reductions and dialect coherently, not as generic markers of native speech.

Edited dialogue need not reproduce every hesitation of a recording. Formality, technical exactness, and restraint can be credible voices too.

#### Pacing through syntax and proportion

Short clauses can create urgency; longer sentences can sustain thought, pressure, or action. Neither length alone determines pace.

Give emotional consequences enough room. Keep transitions proportionate. Remove repeated action, redundant dialogue, and description delaying the scene without changing its effect.

Prepare emotional peaks through relationships, desire, risk, and prior experience. Intense diction cannot replace preparation. Preserve an effective image without immediately explaining it.

Breaks shape attention, pauses, speaker changes, time, and viewpoint. Let variation follow the scene rather than a numerical schedule.

#### Narrative tense and represented speech

Choose a coherent frame. Passé simple can sustain literary past narration; passé composé can support a current or conversational account; present tense creates another perspective. No one tense guarantees quality or nativeness.

Imparfait can supply background, continuing action, habit, or a viewpoint within a past frame. Do not alternate it with completed-event forms merely for variety. Plus-que-parfait can orient earlier events before the frame becomes established.

Dialogue may use a different tense from narration. Direct speech, attributed report, imagined exchange, and free indirect thought need distinct knowledge and temporal boundaries.

Literary subjunctive forms can suit an established historical or formal voice. Do not insert them into every narrative to advertise French elegance. Equally, do not flatten an intentionally sustained voice into casual speech.

#### Anti-cliché openings

| Stock opening | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "Dans un monde où..." | Begin with a character's particular action or predicament. |
| "C'était une nuit sombre et orageuse." | Establish what the weather changes or what the character notices. |
| "Elle était loin de se douter..." | Create dramatic irony through consequential differences in knowledge. |
| "Il était une fois..." | Keep the convention where the tale needs it; otherwise begin with relevant action or detail. |

Do not replace a purposeful fairy-tale form with artificial novelty.

#### Anti-cliché emotion

| Stock wording | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "Un frisson lui parcourut l'échine." | Name a relevant response or action. |
| "Son cœur manqua un battement." | Show what the character does or notices. |
| "Les larmes coulaient sur ses joues." | Use tears when relevant; otherwise find a particular expression or resistance. |
| "Une vague d'émotion la submergea." | Name the emotion or a precise action. |
| "Son sang se glaça." | Establish the fear and its effect. |
| "Elle avait des papillons dans le ventre." | Use a relevant thought, behavior, or perception. |
| "Le temps s'arrêta." | Describe narrowed attention or altered duration. |
| "Son monde s'écroula." | Establish the realization and first consequence. |

These are choices, not a requirement to embody every feeling. Bodily detail can become repetitive, and a familiar idiom can fit a speaker.

#### Anti-cliché description

| Stock wording | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "des yeux bleus perçants" | Choose a detail relevant to the viewpoint. |
| "des cheveux noirs de jais" | Describe consequential appearance, movement, or action. |
| "des traits sculptés" | Give a distinguishing feature where appearance matters. |
| "une femme magnifique" | Name a particular perception or retain a sufficient direct judgment. |
| "Le soleil tapait sans pitié." | Describe what heat changes here. |
| "un silence inquiétant" | Identify a missing or remaining sound and its significance. |
| "La pièce fut plongée dans l'obscurité." | Clarify the change and what remains perceptible. |

Do not invent a scar, asymmetry, smell, or defect solely to distinguish someone.

#### Anti-cliché action

| Stock wording | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "Ce fut le chaos." | Show the first failure or action and its consequence. |
| "Ils se battaient pour leur vie." | Describe a consequential act, mistake, technique, or choice. |
| "à la vitesse de l'éclair" | Give the motion and effect without automatic intensification. |
| "contre toute attente" | Establish the actual expectation or disadvantage. |
| "à la dernière seconde" | Show timing and the consequence of almost being too late. |

Summary remains useful where detailed action is not the focus. Specificity does not require exhaustive choreography.

#### Anti-cliché dialogue

| Stock wording | More particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "Il faut qu'on parle." | Raise the matter unless avoidance is the point. |
| "Tu ne comprends pas." | Make the actual misunderstanding visible. |
| "Ce n'est pas ce que tu crois." | Give the particular explanation, evasion, or lie. |
| "J'ai un mauvais pressentiment." | Identify the observation or conflict behind concern. |
| "Eh bien, c'est réussi !" | Give a response particular to the failure where generic sarcasm adds nothing. |

Familiar lines can belong to particular interactions. Do not make every character conspicuously original.

#### Recurrent generic imagery

| Formula | More direct or particular treatment |
| --- | --- |
| "une tapisserie de..." | Identify elements and relationships. |
| "le poids des souvenirs" | Establish the actual recollection or burden. |
| "naviguer dans les méandres de la vie" | Show choices and constraints. |
| "dans la trame du destin" | State the event or remove generic commentary. |
| "le présage de..." | Identify the sign and what it reasonably predicts. |
| "une cacophonie de..." | Name relevant sounds. |
| "une symphonie de sentiments" | Name feelings or interactions. |
| "la danse des ombres" | Describe movement and its significance. |
| "des promesses murmurées" | State what was said, implied, or hoped for. |
| "un écho du passé" | Identify the memory, reference, or recurring sound. |
| "le tissu de la réalité" | Describe the fictional world's particular change. |
| "Et c'est ainsi que..." | Begin with action unless the narrative form needs the formula. |

Literal meanings remain available. Reusable elevation detached from this scene is the defect.

#### Genre conventions without automatic plots

| Genre | Familiar shortcuts requiring particular causes and consequences |
| --- | --- |
| Fantasy | Automatically chosen orphan; conveniently exact prophecy; evil without motive; uniformly wise elves and gruff dwarves; magic without cost or limits. |
| Science fiction | Immediate hostility following AI sentience; unexamined last-person premise; faster-than-light travel without social consequences; cosmetically different aliens; technology failing only when convenient. |
| Mystery | Surprise based only on the least suspected person; alcoholism as ready-made depth; secret lives replacing development; accidental decisive clues; a villain explaining the plan. |
| Romance | Hostility becoming love without development; body descriptions replacing encounters; decisive misunderstanding sustained by implausible silence; obvious triangle choice; rain kiss as automatic climax. |

A convention can work with particular meaning. Novelty does not repair causality; familiarity does not invalidate a premise. Do not add tragedy, ambiguity, or nonlinear form solely to oppose convention.

#### Choose comparisons from the viewpoint

Draw images from the character's experience. A merchant may understand a threat through debt; a courtier through reputation. The comparison should reveal viewpoint as well as action.

Surprise earns its place through meaning rather than unfamiliar words. Source-language images dependent on idiom or sound may need different French expressions.

Cross-sensory imagery can convey unusual perception; strained comparisons can express an extreme state. Keep them intelligible rather than mixing sensory domains merely to sound poetic.

#### Literary techniques

Choose a technique by its effect. Classical names do not excuse decoration, forced symmetry, or invented logic. Sound and wordplay must work in French.

| Purpose | Available techniques | Useful application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Connect sound | Alliteration, assonance, and consonance repeat initial sounds, vowels, or consonants. | Connect consequential words without conspicuous sound exercises. |
| Shape sonic atmosphere | Sibilance, cacophony, and euphony emphasize hissing, harsh, or smooth combinations. | Support a particular perception. |
| Place sound in a scene | Onomatopoeia uses sound-imitating forms such as "bourdonner", "craquer", and "tinter". | Make consequential sound perceptible without replacing events with effects. |
| Create rhyme-like cadence | Internal rhyme and similar endings connect phrases. | Support ritual, comedy, or musical speech without making narration jingle. |
| Establish a refrain | Anaphora repeats openings; epistrophe endings; symploce both. | Develop concern or insistence. |
| Carry an idea forward | Anadiplosis repeats an ending at the next opening; epanalepsis returns to an opening. | Make continuity or changed implication perceptible. |
| Express insistence | Epizeuxis repeats immediately; diacope separates repeated words. | Convey particular pressure or demand. |
| Characterize through redundancy | Deliberate restatement conveys evasion, resignation, obsession, or ceremony. | Reveal the speaker rather than duplicate information. |
| Develop a word's meaning | Polyptoton varies grammatical form; ploce and antanaclasis repeat with changed senses. | Expose ambiguity or evolving understanding. |
| Articulate contrast | Antithesis balances opposites; paradox and oxymoron express apparent contradiction. | Represent real tension. |
| Reverse a relationship | Chiasmus reverses structures or ideas; antimetabole reverses repeated words. | Make reversal matter to thought. |
| Create balance | Isocolon uses parallel units; a tricolon uses three. | Give a real distinction rhythm without forcing the count. |
| Control emphasis | Periodic sentences delay the assertion; loose sentences give it early. | Match unfolding to expectation or reflection. |
| Control clause movement | Parataxis places clauses together; hypotaxis subordinates them. | Express sequence or dependency. |
| Control accumulation | Asyndeton omits conjunctions; polysyndeton repeats them. | Create urgency, persistence, or exhaustion. |
| Build intensity | Arrange material in increasing force or importance. | Let realization develop instead of starting at maximum intensity. |
| Represent interruption | Aposiopesis breaks off; anacoluthon changes grammatical course. | Express an actual disruption. |
| Compress recoverable information | Ellipsis omits understood words; apposition identifies through an adjacent phrase. | Preserve intelligibility without making explanation telegraphic. |
| Sustain an image | Simile compares explicitly; metaphor implicitly; extended metaphor develops it. | Convey a particular perception. |
| Explain by comparison | Analogy relates domains; allegory extends representation. | Preserve the comparison's limits. |
| Transfer perception | Personification assigns human qualities; transferred epithets shift modifiers. | Reveal viewpoint without asserting literal intentions. |
| Name by association | Synecdoche uses part and whole; metonymy association; merism parts or extremes. | Use intelligible cultural shorthand. |
| Layer knowledge | Dramatic irony gives readers extra knowledge; foreshadowing prepares later events. | Create fair suspense or reinterpretation. |
| Reorder time | Flashback reveals earlier events; flash-forward alters anticipation. | Serve disclosure without losing orientation. |
| Enter directly | In medias res begins amid consequential action. | Establish involvement, then context. |
| Create irony | Verbal irony separates intended and literal meanings; situational irony reverses expectations. | Reveal meaningful discrepancies or relationships, including through an opposite-meaning nickname. |
| Vary emotional scale | Hyperbole exaggerates; litotes and meiosis understate; bathos shifts from important to trivial. | Express attitude or comic reversal. |
| Represent address and desire | Apostrophe addresses the absent or abstract; rhetorical questions express stance. | Give appeals, wishes, and warnings a speaker and stakes. |
| Reveal avoidance | Euphemism softens; dysphemism makes harsher; indirect naming signals taboo. | Characterize without hiding facts in exposition. |
| Draw attention through omission | Mention a subject while professing not to discuss it. | Expose evasion or manipulation. |
| Name through social perception | Epithets, titles, and culturally meaningful names substitute for a person or quality. | Reveal judgment while keeping identity clear. |
| Develop allusion | Refer to works, myths, events, or associations. | Supply context; obscurity is not depth. |
| Use wordplay | Puns exploit sound or meaning; zeugma and syllepsis give a governing word different applications. | Create French wit rather than an unworkable translated pun. |
| Build vocabulary | Neologisms coin terms; portmanteaus blend them; kennings compress metaphorical names. | Name distinctions a culture needs. |
| Shape diction | Category shifts, paired nouns expressing one idea, and word insertions distinguish utterances. | Keep unusual formation intelligible and character-specific. |
| Represent verbal mistakes | Malapropisms substitute similar words; spoonerisms transpose sounds. | Characterize without stereotypes. |
| Focus description | Develop artwork, place, era, season, appearance, or behavior. | Give relevant attention; inventories may express fixation. |
| Accumulate detail | Enumeratio lists; amplification develops; distributio assigns roles or consequences. | Establish scope without perpetual inventory. |
| Reveal moving thought | Aporia expresses doubt; self-correction revises a formulation. | Make changed judgment intelligible. |
| Reveal reasoning | Anticipate objections, eliminate alternatives, or derive contradictions. | Supply a basis; unsupported slippery slopes are not proofs. |
| Constrain choice | Alternatives carry different costs or dangers. | Establish actual constraints without false binaries. |
| Expose assumptions | Conclusions rely on premises treated as shared. | Reveal worldview; factual argument still needs defensible premises. |
| Represent imagined speech | Rehearse conversations or imagine replies. | Distinguish anticipation from actual exchange. |
| Express worldview concisely | Maxims or pointed observations state particular understanding. | Fit the speaker without automatic lessons. |
| Return to earlier ideas | Closing images or phrases recall prior material. | Earn closure through changed meaning. |

Sound, repetition, and syntax can support one moment. Avoid stacking devices until construction overwhelms experience. Quiet connective passages can sustain the larger work.

#### Necessary literary exceptions

Metaphor, repetition, fragments, unusual order, nonstandard grammar, direct address, questions, and exclamations can serve specific characters or effects. Their justification is a concrete function such as pressure, disruption, cultural language, or viewpoint.

Proverbs, curses, and strategic compliments should belong to a speaker and situation. Do not replace ordinary conversation everywhere with conspicuous originality.

Keep dialect coherent and respectful. Deliberate speech deviations do not require errors in narration. Exceptions do not authorize generic bodily metaphors, philosophical speeches, nonlinear scenes, or flourishes throughout.

Represent interruptions through an appropriate construction, action, or break without decorative sentence dashes. Exact quoted documents and required embedded notation retain their punctuation.

#### Worked example

> Before: "Une vague de tristesse la submergea lorsqu'elle comprit que le vrai courage consiste parfois à lâcher prise. Son cœur souffrait sous le poids des souvenirs."
>
> Illustrative scene: "Elle glissa la clé de secours dans l'enveloppe. Pendant un moment, elle garda la main dessus."

The alternative invents a particular action and leaves its emotional implication available. It is not a paraphrase of every grief. A direct label can suit another scene better. Preserve the treatment serving this character and moment without explaining an already intelligible implication.
